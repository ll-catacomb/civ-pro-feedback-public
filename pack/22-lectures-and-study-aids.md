## § 1292(a)(1): Interlocutory Appeals of Injunctions

We examined 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), which provides appellate jurisdiction for orders granting, modifying, refusing to grant, or refusing to continue an injunction.

The underlying logic has two sides:

- **Defendant-side**: If a court is going to impose affirmative obligations on a defendant, the defendant should have an immediate appeal to test whether that ruling is legally correct.
- **Plaintiff-side**: If a plaintiff has alleged irreparable harm and the injunction is denied, there is a presumptive emergency that warrants immediate appellate review to determine whether that harm should be prevented.

---

## § 1292(b): The Double Discretion Doctrine

[00:05:00] Section 1292(b) establishes a two-step discretionary mechanism for interlocutory appeals.

**Step 1 — District court certification.** The district judge must enter a written order finding that:
1. The prior order decided a controlling issue of law;
2. There is substantial ground for difference of opinion on that issue; and
3. An immediate appeal would materially advance the ultimate resolution of the litigation.

**Step 2 — Circuit court acceptance.** Within 10 days of that certification, the losing party must petition the Court of Appeals. The court of appeals has complete discretion to accept or decline. Factors include the procedural posture of the case, the costs the parties are incurring, and the court's own docket. The circuits exercise very wide discretion here.

---

## The Cohen Collateral Order Doctrine

The collateral order doctrine provides appellate jurisdiction for certain interlocutory orders. An order qualifies when it:
1. **Finally decides** the particular issue;
2. Is **collateral to the merits** of the litigation; and
3. Is **effectively unreviewable later**.

The third element is where most collateral order claims fail. The Supreme Court is very reluctant to find that issues are effectively unreviewable on later appeal. Examples the Court has rejected include denials of motions to transfer or dismiss on forum non-convenience grounds, and denials of protective orders based on privilege.

Examples the Court has accepted include the issue in *Cohen* itself — whether a litigation bond is required — and abstention-based stays.

---

## Petition for Writ of Mandamus

[00:15:00] A writ of mandamus is technically a new legal proceeding in the Court of Appeals — a lawsuit against the district judge — asking the appellate court to order the judge to take a specific action. It functions like an appeal but is not technically one.

**Two categories:**

1. **Supervisory mandamus** — illustrated by the *LaBuy* litigation, where the Court of Appeals observed a district court repeatedly referring cases for trial to special masters and directed the district judge to stop, holding that the judge must personally hear cases.

2. **Emergency mandamus** — far more common of the two. The petitioner must show:
   - Irreparable harm will result if the court waits for a traditional appeal;
   - A clear and indisputable right to relief; and
   - That issuing the writ would be a wise exercise of the court's discretion given all relevant circumstances.

These factors closely track the preliminary injunction standard, and courts asked to issue emergency mandamus often ask: Is this effectively reviewable later? What are the consequences? How certain is the right to relief?

---

## Expanding the Lawsuit Beyond a Single Plaintiff vs. Single Defendant

[00:30:00] With appellate jurisdiction concluded, we turned to expanding the lawsuit. The most important structural point: **to move beyond a single plaintiff versus a single defendant, you must run a two-step analysis.**

1. **Federal rules permission**: Is it permissible under the Federal Rules to accumulate additional claims or additional parties?
2. **Jurisdictional analysis**: Is there subject matter jurisdiction, personal jurisdiction, venue, and any other required predicate — analyzed claim by claim and party by party?

### Multiple Plaintiffs and Defendants

Under Rule 20, multiple plaintiffs may join if their claims arise from the same transaction or occurrence and share a common issue of law or fact — a low threshold.

### Counterclaims: Rule 13

Whenever there is an arrow pointing one way (a claim from one party to another), Rule 13 applies:
- **Compulsory counterclaim**: If the claim arises from the same transaction or occurrence as the original claim, the defending party *must* assert it or forfeit it once the first case goes to judgment.
- **Permissive counterclaim**: Any other claim the defending party holds against the opposing party; it need not be asserted.

The loss of a compulsory counterclaim triggers when the first case reaches trial court judgment — that is when preclusion rules attach.

### Cross-Claims

Cross-claims (Rule 13(g)) are always permissive and require the same transaction or occurrence. Claims by plaintiffs invoking supplemental jurisdiction warrant particular attention: § 1367(b) restricts supplemental jurisdiction for certain plaintiff-side claims even when the federal rules would permit them.

### Third-Party Complaints: Rule 14

Third-party practice does not invoke the transaction-or-occurrence test. Instead, it operates on a theory of **shifted liability** — most commonly when a defendant brings in its insurer, saying: "If I have to pay, you have to pay." This is the one arrow that does not depend on transaction or occurrence.

### Rule 18: Claim Joinder

Once any claim exists between two parties, Rule 18 allows the asserting party to bring any additional claim against the opposing party whatsoever — even claims completely unrelated to the original. This promotes efficiency and allows parties to bargain all their claims against each other at once. Rule 42 then empowers the district court to restructure the litigation into coherent pieces as it sees fit.

---

## The Transaction-or-Occurrence Test: *Heyward-Robinson*

[00:45:00] A quick walkthrough of *Heyward-Robinson* <!-- corrected: "Hayward Robinson" → "Heyward-Robinson" --> illustrates how broadly federal courts interpret the transaction-or-occurrence test.

Because of a quirk in pre-§ 1367 supplemental jurisdiction law (which you need not know), the court had to determine whether two separate construction contracts and jobsites arose from the same transaction or occurrence. Despite being geographically separated, the court found they did because:

- The contracts were negotiated at roughly the same time;
- They contained **cross-termination** and **cross-default clauses** — a default on one gave the non-breaching party the right to cancel both;
- Payments were not differentiated between the two contracts, showing the parties treated them as a single arrangement;
- Work on both jobs was ongoing simultaneously; and
- A single insurance policy guaranteed performance under both contracts.

The general trend in federal courts is to read "transaction or occurrence" broadly to encompass any logical relationship or relationship in time, space, or evidentiary overlap — not without limit, but generously.

This test will be central when we turn to claim preclusion, where "same claim" is essentially coextensive with "same transaction or occurrence."

## Claim Preclusion: Finality and the Merits Requirement

[00:00:00] We finished the black letter law of claim preclusion, focusing on element three: a judgment that is both **final** and **on the merits**.

**Finality** for claim preclusion purposes attaches at the trial court judgment — even if the case is on appeal, the trial court judgment is sufficient to trigger preclusion.

**"On the merits"** is a policy concept rather than a literal description. It reflects a judgment about when we want to prevent a second lawsuit on the same transaction or occurrence by the same party. The following are *on the merits*:
- Judgment after trial
- Grant of summary judgment
- Successful motion to dismiss (ordinarily)
- Dismissal for failure to prosecute (because we do not want to allow the plaintiff to refile as a matter of policy)
- In some jurisdictions: limitations-based dismissal

The following are *not* on the merits:
- Dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction
- Dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction
- Dismissal for improper venue
- In some jurisdictions: limitations-based dismissal

---

## Additional Claim Preclusion Doctrines

### The *Martino* Rule <!-- corrected: "Mahreze" → "Martino" -->

When the rendering court in lawsuit number one lacks the **capacity** — especially the jurisdictional capacity — to adjudicate a particular theory of relief, that theory will *not* be claim precluded in a subsequent suit. In other words, a court cannot preclude what it could not have adjudicated. We saw this where the rendering court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over the theory asserted in the second lawsuit; that theory was not barred.

### Court Limitations on Preclusive Effect

A rendering court may limit the claim-preclusive effect of its own judgment by specifying that the adjudication is **without prejudice** to a refiling of a particular theory of relief.

---

## Issue Preclusion

[00:15:00] We then moved to issue preclusion. The following structural points govern:

- **Finality requirement**: Ordinarily a trial court judgment is needed for issue preclusion to run.
- **Insufficient opportunity or incentive**: If a party was prevented from fully litigating an issue in lawsuit one — due to limits on witness testimony, document access, or because the amount at stake was too small to justify full investment — the court has discretion to deny issue preclusion.
- **Due process constraint**: We only preclude parties who were present in lawsuit one or who were in **privity** with those who were.
- **Only losers are precluded**: Issue preclusion runs only against the party who *lost* the issue in lawsuit one. This is easy to forget as fact patterns grow complex.

---

## Elements of Issue Preclusion

### Element 1: Same Issue

Issues are defined **narrowly**. In *Cromwell*, for example, the issues were: Did Mr. Cromwell buy the bonds before maturity? Did he pay value for them? Did he buy them without knowledge of any underlying fraud? Each is a discrete factual question. Issues are narrower than theories of relief and much narrower than transaction occurrences.

### Element 2: Actually Litigated

Issue preclusion runs only if the issue was **actually litigated** in lawsuit one. Unlike compulsory counterclaims under Rule 13, parties are not required to litigate all possible issues. If an issue was not raised and contested, issue preclusion will not run even if the party had the opportunity to raise it.

### Element 3: Necessary to the Decision

[00:25:00] We examined two scenarios from *Russell v. Place*. <!-- corrected: "Russell versus Place" → *Russell v. Place* --> When a jury verdict rests on **multiple independent grounds** and it is impossible to determine which ground the jury relied upon:

- **Federal rule**: The party seeking issue preclusion cannot prove the issue was "necessary to the decision" and issue preclusion will not run.
- **Some state jurisdictions**: Courts will deem *all* independent grounds to have been decided against the losing party, so issue preclusion runs on each.

In *Russell v. Place* itself, the jury could have based its finding on the fat-liquor process or the tanned lambskin; <!-- corrected: "tan lamb skin" → tanned lambskin --> since it was impossible to say which, the federal rule precluded issue preclusion.

---

## Preview: Mutuality (Tomorrow's Topic)

[00:35:00] We close with a preview of mutuality for issue preclusion — the question of whether the parties in lawsuit two must bear some required relationship to the parties in lawsuit one.

**Four frameworks to be covered:**

1. **Strict mutuality**: Both the plaintiff and defendant in lawsuit two must be the same parties, in the same procedural roles, as in lawsuit one.

2. **Relaxed mutuality**: The parties must be the same, but procedural roles may be reversed — the plaintiff in lawsuit one may be the defendant in lawsuit two.

3. **Federal common law rule**: **Defensive non-mutual issue preclusion** is generally permitted. When plaintiff sues defendant A, loses on an issue, and then sues defendant B, defendant B may invoke issue preclusion against the plaintiff on that same issue. However, **offensive non-mutual issue preclusion** — where plaintiff two uses a prior loss by the defendant against the defendant — is generally *not* permitted under federal common law, unless plaintiff two could not have joined lawsuit one.

4. **New York rule** (*Hart*): There is no mutuality requirement at all.

## Overview

This is the summary for the first day of Civil Procedure (Sections 2 and 3). We covered the rules governing when a case can begin in the federal court system.

---

## The Federal Court System and the Choice of Forum

[00:00:00] We began with the basic structural chart: the state court system on the left (trial court → appellate court → state high court) and the federal court system on the right (U.S. District Court → U.S. Court of Appeals → U.S. Supreme Court). The state court system is always willing to hear any case. The question is when the plaintiff also has the option to file in federal district court.

A plaintiff has a choice of federal court only when the **United States District Court has subject matter jurisdiction** over the dispute.

---

## The Constitutional and Statutory Framework

The Constitution grants Congress the power to confer jurisdiction on the lower federal courts. That constitutional power is broad. But unless and until Congress actually enacts a statute conferring jurisdiction, the lower federal courts cannot exercise it.

Visually: there is a large oval representing the outer limit of constitutional power, and a smaller oval inside it representing the actual statutory grants Congress has made. For a case to begin in federal court, it must fall within the smaller, statutory oval — not just the constitutional one.

---

## § 1331: Arising Under Jurisdiction

[00:15:00] The first statutory basis is **28 U.S.C. § 1331**, which grants jurisdiction over cases "arising under the laws of the United States."

Despite the fact that identical "arising under" language in the Constitution is read broadly, the Supreme Court in *Motley* interprets § 1331 narrowly: arising under jurisdiction exists only when the plaintiff's **cause of action** is federal — meaning the right to sue was created by federal law.

**Key rule from *Motley* — the well-pleaded complaint rule**: The federal question must appear on the face of the plaintiff's well-pleaded complaint. Federal issues that arise only as anticipated defenses or in response to defenses do not suffice.

In *Motley* itself, the plaintiffs brought a **state law breach of contract** claim against a railroad that had refused to honor free-pass contracts. There were federal issues lurking in the case, but they arose only as defenses — not as part of the cause of action itself. The contract existed, the railroad breached it, and the harm was clear — all without reference to any federal issue. The Supreme Court dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, even though neither party had raised the issue and the case had already proceeded to the Supreme Court.

**Structural rules from *Motley*:**
1. The constitutional grant of judicial power is a grant to Congress, not directly to the courts.
2. The lower federal courts can only hear cases within the statutory grant.
3. A federal court has an **independent duty** to assess its own subject matter jurisdiction — it must dismiss *sua sponte* even if both parties argue that jurisdiction exists. This obligation never expires; it persists on appeal.

**Exclusive federal jurisdiction**: A subset of arising-under cases — primarily federal antitrust, patents and copyrights, and suits against the United States — must be filed in federal court. Congress has designated these as exclusive.

*Historical note*: Until 1875, there was no general arising under statute. Federal causes of action created between 1790 and 1875 had to be initiated in state court.

---

## The Smith-Grable Exception

[00:35:00] *Motley* would be clean law if it were the end of the story. It is not. Even when a plaintiff's cause of action is created by state law, there may still be arising under jurisdiction under § 1331 if a federal issue is necessarily embedded in the state law claim.

We began discussion of *Smith*. There, shareholders used a **state law shareholder's derivative action** — a state-created cause of action — alleging that the corporation's directors and officers had damaged the corporation by investing in United States government bonds that were allegedly unconstitutional and therefore worthless.

The structure is:
- **Cause of action**: State law (shareholder's derivative suit alleging breach of fiduciary duty)
- **Federal issue**: Whether Congress had the power to issue the bonds — i.e., whether those bonds were valid under the Constitution

The federal issue is not incidental. It is embedded within the state law claim: there is no way to prove that the directors and officers did something wrong without first establishing that the bonds are worthless, which requires resolving the federal constitutional question.

This contrasts directly with *Motley*, where the breach-of-contract cause of action — contract formed, contract breached, harm suffered — could be fully proved without ever encountering the federal issue. The federal issue entered *Motley* only through the railroad's defense.

# SOURCE: content/course/lectures-videos/class-02-summary.md — Subject Matter Jurisdiction: Arising Under, Smith-Grable, Declaratory Judgment, and Diversity

## Overview: Subject Matter Jurisdiction

We are continuing our discussion of subject matter jurisdiction of the federal courts. Subject matter jurisdiction doctrine determines when a case can begin in federal court — in other words, when a plaintiff has the choice of whether to proceed in federal court or state court.

## The Venn Diagram: Constitutional Power vs. Statutory Power

- **State court only** — some cases fall outside the constitutional power entirely and must proceed in state court.
- **Constitutional power** — a broad but not unlimited grant of power from Article III to Congress. Congress may use or decline to use this power by passing a statute.
- **Statutory ovals** — Congress has passed two statutes that we now know:
  - **28 U.S.C. § 1331** — arising under jurisdiction
  - **28 U.S.C. § 1332** — diversity jurisdiction

Both statutory ovals sit entirely within the larger constitutional oval. To properly lie in federal district court, a case must fall within one of the statutory ovals. It is not enough to fall within the broader constitutional power alone.

Despite the fact that both statutes track the language of the Constitution, courts have interpreted the statutes more narrowly than the Constitution.

## Arising Under Jurisdiction (§ 1331): The Well-Pleaded Complaint Rule

Under § 1331, for almost all cases, courts apply the **well-pleaded complaint rule**: there is arising under jurisdiction only if the plaintiff's cause of action is a federally created cause of action.

The constitutional standard is broader — any federal issue embedded in a case would suffice — but that is not enough under the statute as interpreted in *Mottley*. <!-- corrected: "Motley" → "Mottley" -->

## The Smith-Grable Exception

An important exception to the rule that only a federally created cause of action will do under § 1331 is the **Smith-Grable exception**.

The doctrine has two steps:

**Step 1 — Power:** Does a federal issue exist that is embedded within the state law cause of action, and is that issue both *substantial* and *disputed*?

**Step 2 — Discretion:** If power exists, should the federal court exercise arising under jurisdiction? This is a multifactor balancing inquiry.

### *Smith*

In *Smith*, the case was a shareholder's derivative lawsuit. The theory was that directors and officers had caused the corporation to invest in federal government bonds that were in fact worthless because the federal government lacked the constitutional authority to issue them. The constitutionality of the bonds was embedded within the state law cause of action — there was no way to prove the claim without resolving whether the bonds were invalid.

The federal issue was:
- **Embedded** within the state law cause of action
- **Substantial** — federal bond issuance is critical to how the federal government finances itself
- **Disputed** — the parties would fight over it

On the discretion factors: the constitutionality of federal bonds would not arise frequently enough in shareholders' derivative suits to sweep excessive business from the state courts into the federal system. It was also a clean issue of law, not entangled with specific facts.

### *Grable*

In *Grable*, the cause of action was one for quiet title. The prior landowner's theory depended on the claim that the IRS's tax sale to the new landowner was invalid due to a failure to follow federal statutory notice requirements. There was no way to litigate the quiet title claim without adjudicating that federal statute.

The federal issue was:
- **Embedded** within the quiet title cause of action
- **Substantial** — the case would turn on it, and it implicates IRS tax collection and administration
- **Disputed** — a central issue in the case

On the discretion factors: the IRS notice question would not arise frequently enough in quiet title actions to flood the federal courts. It was again a clean issue of law — whether the IRS was required to serve process in a particular manner.

The court held that both *Smith* and *Grable* were appropriate for the exception.

### *Merrell Dow* — A Contrast <!-- corrected: "Merrill Dow" → "Merrell Dow" -->

In *Merrell Dow*, the theory was that a violation of federal law formed the basis of a state law negligence action (the concept you will encounter in Torts as *negligence per se*). Even if the federal issue were embedded within the state negligence claim, allowing any violation of federal law to form the basis for a state negligence action would sweep far too many cases into the federal court system. The best way to understand *Merrell Dow* is that exercising arising under jurisdiction would have upset the balance of federal-state business too dramatically — the discretion step fails.

## Declaratory Judgment Statute

The federal declaratory judgment statute is straightforward if you remember one thing: **a declaratory judgment is a remedy, not a source of jurisdiction.**

The analytical approach is to imagine the *coercive action* — the lawsuit that would have been filed in the absence of the declaratory judgment statute. Ask: would that imaginary lawsuit have fallen within one of the two statutory ovals (§ 1331 or § 1332)?

- If yes, the declaratory judgment action may proceed in federal court.
- If no, there is no subject matter jurisdiction.

Note: Smith-Grable is available as a basis for § 1331 jurisdiction in the declaratory judgment context if needed.

**Example — "Mr. Schmoe":** The coercive action would have been a breach of contract lawsuit by Mr. Schmoe against the bank, in which the federal issue arose only by way of defense. This is the identical posture to *Mottley* — a breach of contract claim with a federal issue arising defensively, which does not support § 1331 jurisdiction. Accordingly, when the bank tried to seize the forum by filing a declaratory judgment action claiming it was not liable, the court dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.

## Diversity Jurisdiction (§ 1332)

### Complete Diversity

Under *Strawbridge v. Curtiss*, the diversity statute ordinarily requires **complete diversity**: all plaintiffs must be citizens of different states from all defendants.

The Constitution requires only *minimal diversity* (at least one plaintiff diverse from at least one defendant), but that is not sufficient under the statute.

**Examples:**
- Two plaintiffs from South Carolina suing one defendant from Virginia and one from North Carolina → **complete diversity** ✓
- Two plaintiffs from South Carolina suing one defendant from Virginia and one from South Carolina → **no complete diversity** ✗ (South Carolina appears on both sides)

### Citizenship Rules

**Corporations** — Citizens of:
1. Their state of incorporation, *and*
2. Their **principal place of business** — the nerve center where corporate decision-making is conducted (not the location of primary manufacturing, sales, or other activity)

**Unincorporated associations** — Citizens of every state in which *any* of their members are citizens. This often makes unincorporated associations citizens of a large number of states.

**Individuals** — The relevant concept is **domicile**:
- Peg domicile at birth, then move forward chronologically.
- Domicile changes only if the person is (1) residing in a new state *and* (2) has the intention to remain there **indefinitely** (no definite leaving point).
- Evidence of domicile: driver's license, tax filings, marriage, relationships, purchase of property, etc.
- Classic non-changes: students and employees with a definite end date do not change domicile.

### Amount in Controversy

Diversity jurisdiction requires the amount in controversy to exceed **$75,000**. There is no amount in controversy requirement for § 1331 arising under jurisdiction.

The court accepts the plaintiff's stated amount unless it can conclude to a **legal certainty** that the claim cannot exceed $75,000. Various tests apply when the plaintiff does not seek a specific monetary amount — these are secondary issues best addressed through practice.

## Overview

Class 4 covered removal procedure and the doctrine of abstention, with particular focus on *Colorado River* abstention.

---

## Removal: Basic Mechanics

[00:00:00] Removal is the mechanism by which a defendant moves a case originally filed in state court to federal district court.

**Automatic removal**: Removal takes effect the instant a defendant files the removal papers in federal court. What we are really examining, therefore, is whether a **motion to remand** will be granted — i.e., whether the case will be sent back to state court.

**Remand for lack of subject matter jurisdiction**: At any time, and on the court's own initiative (*sua sponte*), a district court that determines it lacks subject matter jurisdiction must remand the case. This is an extension of *Motley*: when a removed case lacks a jurisdictional basis, the court remands rather than dismisses.

---

## Procedural Defects in Removal: Grounds for Remand

[00:08:00] Beyond the subject-matter jurisdiction ground, a plaintiff may file a **prompt motion to remand** — within 30 days of removal — based on any of the following procedural defects:

### 1. In-State Defendant Rule (Diversity Cases Only)

In a case where subject matter jurisdiction rests solely on § 1332 (diversity), if **any defendant is a citizen of the state in which the lawsuit was filed**, removal is improper. The rationale: diversity jurisdiction is designed to protect against potential bias against out-of-state parties. If a defendant is from the forum state, there is no basis to fear bias against defendants, and the plaintiff's choice of that state court is entitled to respect.

### 2. Late Removal (More Than 30 Days After the Removable Event)

Removal must occur within 30 days of the **removable event** — ordinarily the filing of a complaint that facially establishes a basis for federal jurisdiction. The rule promotes prompt decision-making about forum.

Sometimes, events during litigation *create* removability where none existed before. Two primary examples:
- Dismissal of non-diverse parties in a potential diversity case
- State court permission to add a federally-created cause of action to a complaint that previously lacked one

### 3. Failure of All Defendants to Join the Removal Petition

All defendants must sign the removal petition. The federal court system is intended to be the exception, not the rule, for trial-level litigation. If any defendant wants to remain in state court, the case stays there.

### 4. The One-Year Rule (Diversity Cases Only)

In diversity-only cases, if the case has been pending in state court for **more than one year** (measured from filing and service, which we treat as occurring on the same day), removal is untimely. This applies only when § 1332 is the sole jurisdictional basis.

---

## The Shamrock Oil Rule

From *Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets*: <!-- corrected: "Shamrock Oil" → *Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets* --> **Plaintiffs cannot remove.** Counterclaims and cross-claims are irrelevant to removability. We look only to the plaintiff's complaint. Even if a defendant asserts an arising-under counterclaim and the plaintiff attempts to remove, the district court will remand on prompt motion.

---

## Abstention: When a Federal Court Declines to Exercise Jurisdiction

[00:25:00] Abstention doctrines govern circumstances in which a federal district court, despite having all necessary jurisdictional predicates (subject matter jurisdiction, and the personal jurisdiction and venue we will study in the coming weeks), nonetheless refuses to go forward. When, if ever, is that legitimate?

**Standing categorical exceptions** (not subject to a balancing test): Federal district courts will not issue divorce decrees and will not probate wills. The stated rationale is state court expertise, though one could argue circularity — the federal courts' longstanding refusal to hear these matters has created the very state court expertise invoked to justify the refusal.

---

## Colorado River Abstention

The abstention doctrine we must know is the **fourth branch** of abstention doctrine, from *Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States*. <!-- corrected: "Colorado River" → *Colorado River Water Conservation District v. United States* -->

**Threshold requirement**: Simultaneous state court and federal court litigation must be pending between substantially the same parties over substantially the same subject matter.

**Default rule — the unflagging obligation**: Even when that condition is met, federal district courts are under an **unflagging obligation** to exercise the jurisdiction Congress has conferred on them. Absent exceptional circumstances, they must proceed.

**When abstention is permitted**: Only in **exceptional circumstances** may a district court stay (ordinarily not dismiss, but stay) the federal litigation. The analysis is a totality-of-the-circumstances inquiry. Factors the *Colorado River* Court found relevant included:
- Presence of a congressional statute suggesting congressional comfort with state court adjudication
- Relatively rapid progress of the state court litigation
- The state court's investment of time and resources in a complex multi-party proceeding
- State court expertise developed through that investment
- Geographic convenience of the state court for parties and evidence

**Circuit split on "exceptional circumstances"**: The circuits are split. In the Seventh Circuit, following *Clark v. Lacy*, <!-- corrected: "Clark v. Lacey" → *Clark v. Lacy* --> Colorado River abstention stays are relatively easy to obtain — parallel state and federal litigation is treated as presumptively appropriate for abstention. In other circuits, such as the Fourth, it is very difficult to obtain a stay. The Supreme Court has not resolved this split.

**Background effect**: When parallel state and federal trial court litigation is simultaneously pending, the first one to reach a trial court judgment will ordinarily trigger preclusion doctrines that terminate the other lawsuit.

## Overview

Class 5 covered horizontal choice of law (which state's substantive law governs when more than one state's law could apply) and introduced the foundational concepts of personal jurisdiction.

---

## Horizontal Choice of Law: The Basic Framework

[00:00:00] When attacking a choice-of-law problem — on an exam, in practice, or on an assignment — begin with a threshold question:

**Is the issue procedural or substantive?**

- **Procedural issues**: Apply the law of the **forum** — the state in which the lawsuit is occurring. This applies to all procedural questions without further analysis.
- **Substantive issues**: Conduct a choice-of-law analysis to determine which state's law governs.

---

## Procedure vs. Substance: Key Examples

Some issues fall clearly on one side:

- **Procedural**: Paper size, filing deadlines, response deadlines.
- **Limitations periods**: For our purposes, treat limitations periods as procedural. This means the forum state applies its own limitations period — even if the events occurred in another state with a shorter or longer period. A plaintiff who can find a forum with a longer limitations period may file there even if the period in the state where the events occurred has already run. Conversely, if the forum's limitations period is shorter than the period in the state where the events arose, the forum will apply its own shorter period and the claim may be time-barred.

---

## Tort Choice of Law: Two Approaches

We studied tort choice of law only. Contracts, property, and family law choice of law are not covered in this course. On any exam or assignment, you will be told whether the relevant jurisdiction follows the First or Second Restatement.

### First Restatement: *Lex Loci Delicti*

Apply the law of the **place of the injury** — where the damage occurred and the tort was complete. For personal injury, identify where the blood is. For intangible injuries like defamation (an injury to reputation), you must work out where the reputation is located, which can be harder.

Once you identify the place of injury, that state's law governs **all** substantive tort issues: damages caps, liability rules, culpability standards, and relationship-based defenses like guest statutes and fellow-servant rules.

**Strengths of the First Restatement approach:**
- Hard-and-fast rules that are easy and cheap to administer
- Predictability — parties can reckon the applicable law in advance
- Internal coherence — a single jurisdiction's complete body of tort law applies

### Second Restatement

Two key differences from the First Restatement:

1. **Issue-by-issue analysis**: The lawsuit is disaggregated into individual issues, and different states' laws may govern different issues. New York law might govern one issue; South Carolina law another; Texas law a third.

2. **Presumption with escape valve**: For each issue, begin with a presumption in favor of *lex loci delicti* (the First Restatement rule). Then examine the **§ 6 factors** — including the relevant policies of the forum, the relevant policies of other interested states, the parties' reasonable expectations, and the needs of the interstate judicial system — to decide whether to override the presumption.

*Babcock v. Jackson*: The tort occurred in Ontario, which had a guest statute. Under *lex loci delicti*, Ontario law would govern. But the court concluded that New York had the dominant interest in the guest-statute issue because the driver, passenger, and their insurance were all New York-based. Ontario's purpose in enacting the guest statute — protecting drivers' insurers against passenger-driver fraud — was a New York concern when the parties were New York residents with New York insurance. The court therefore applied New York law on that issue. However, had the issue been whether the driver's conduct was sufficiently careless to constitute negligence, Ontario law would govern because Ontario has a strong interest in regulating conduct within its borders.

**Danger of the Second Restatement**: *Frankenstein law* — when too many pieces of different states' laws are stitched together, the resulting legal scheme may be one that no jurisdiction would ever have adopted as a whole.

## *Pennoyer v. Neff* and the Territorial Vision

[00:00:00] In *Pennoyer*, the Court articulates a strong territorial theory of personal jurisdiction: a state may exercise personal jurisdiction only over (1) a citizen of that state, (2) a corporation incorporated or resident there, or (3) an individual physically tagged with process **while present in the state**.

The dual rationale: state sovereigns should not encroach on each other's territorial authority, and a defendant tagged in-state could not complain too loudly about the inconvenience of litigating there — in an era of difficult travel, if you got there at all, it probably was not too burdensome to stay.

---

## The Pressure on *Pennoyer*: Cars, Contracts, and Corporations

As the national economy developed — contracts formed by mail and telegraph, corporations operating across state lines, railroads, telephones, and eventually automobiles — the in-state tag requirement became untenable. Courts developed fictions: treating state officials as appointed agents for service, deeming corporations "present" in a state simply by virtue of doing business there.

*International Shoe Co. v. Washington* casts aside those fictions and replaces them with a focus on the **nature and quality of the defendant's contacts** with the forum as the measure of whether jurisdiction is permissible.

---

## *International Shoe*: Two Types of Personal Jurisdiction

The *International Shoe* Court recognized two distinct bases of personal jurisdiction:

1. **General jurisdiction**: Jurisdiction over the defendant for any claim whatsoever, regardless of whether the subject matter of the litigation has any connection to the forum.
2. **Specific jurisdiction**: Jurisdiction that depends on the relationship among the defendant's activities, the subject matter of the litigation, and the forum.

*International Shoe* was itself a **specific jurisdiction** case. The company sent salespeople into Washington to solicit shoe orders. The litigation concerned unpaid unemployment insurance taxes on exactly that activity. The connection between what the defendant did in the forum and what the lawsuit was about was direct.

The majority opinion focuses almost exclusively on the defendant's conduct and contacts. It does not address the state's interest in providing a forum, the plaintiff's interest in a convenient place to litigate, the location of evidence, or which law will apply. Those factors will enter the analysis later.

---

## General Personal Jurisdiction

[00:20:00] Three bases (with a possible fourth):

### 1. "Really at Home" — Domicile and Daimler

An individual can be sued in the state of their **domicile** for any claim at all. For corporations, the parallel is **principal place of business** and **state of incorporation** — a corporation can be sued on any basis in either of those states.

This requires no long-arm statute because the defendant is at home and the court's power is not contested on due process grounds.

### 2. Daimler "At Home" Jurisdiction

From *Daimler AG v. Bauman*: a defendant's activities in a state may be so substantial relative to its total activities that the forum is **highly analogous** to the defendant's actual home. Because the defendant is not literally at home, a **long-arm statute** authorizing the court to hear the case is required, plus the exercise must satisfy the constitutional standard.

Long-arm statutes may: (a) enumerate specific circumstances in which jurisdiction is authorized; (b) authorize jurisdiction to the constitutional limits; or (c) both. If a case falls outside the enumerated circumstances and the statute does not authorize jurisdiction to constitutional limits, the court lacks statutory authority regardless of the constitutional analysis.

After *Daimler*, this form of general jurisdiction is rare. What is required is that a **large fraction of the defendant's total activities** (not merely a large absolute volume) occur in the forum state.

### 3. Consent

A defendant can waive personal jurisdiction and consent to it. Consent is established when:
- The defendant **fails to object promptly** — typically in the first responsive filing (a motion to dismiss or in the answer); a defendant who does not timely raise the objection forfeits it.
- The **plaintiff** consents to personal jurisdiction — in the forum where plaintiff filed — by virtue of filing there. A plaintiff cannot then object to personal jurisdiction over them for a counterclaim filed in that forum, even a counterclaim with no relationship to the forum and one that could not have been filed there originally.
- A **forum selection clause** constitutes advance consent. Unless the chosen forum is extremely oppressive, courts enforce forum selection clauses. A party who agreed to a clause cannot later object to being sued in the designated forum.

### (Possible Fourth Basis: The In-State Tag)

In *Pennoyer*, in-state physical service was sufficient to establish general personal jurisdiction. After *Burnham v. Superior Court*, four justices take this seriously and four do not. The status of the in-state tag for general jurisdiction remains unresolved, and you should be prepared to argue both ways.

## Federal Long-Arm Statute: Rule 4(k)(1)(A)

[00:00:00] Last class we discussed state long-arm statutes. Federal long-arm statutes work differently.

**Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4(k)(1)(A)**: A federal court may exercise personal jurisdiction over a defendant whenever a court of the state in which the federal court is sitting could do so. It is precisely that simple — and that limiting. If the relevant state court could not exercise personal jurisdiction (either because the state long-arm statute does not reach the defendant, or because the Constitution blocks it), the federal court under 4(k)(1)(A) cannot exercise jurisdiction either.

---

## Building the Two-Phase Framework

[00:08:00] *McGee* expanded the analysis beyond the defendant's contacts alone. *Hanson v. Denckla* then imposed a constraint, and *World-Wide Volkswagen* confirmed the two-phase structure.

### Phase 1: Minimum Contacts (2A on the Framework)

Ask whether there are **minimum contacts** linking:
- the defendant,
- the subject matter of the litigation, and
- the forum.

The key element within minimum contacts is **purposeful availment**: did the defendant deliberately direct its activities toward the forum, seek to benefit from the forum, and avail itself of the forum's law and protections?

If minimum contacts are **absent**, the analysis ends. It does not matter how strong the plaintiff's interest is, how convenient the forum is for the state, or how logical it would be to litigate there. No minimum contacts = no jurisdiction.

### Phase 2: Fair and Reasonable (2B on the Framework)

If minimum contacts are present, the court then considers whether exercise of jurisdiction would be **fair and reasonable**, weighing:
- Extent of the defendant's contacts (beyond the threshold minimum)
- Defendant's convenience (the burden of litigating in the forum)
- Plaintiff's interest and convenience
- The forum state's interest in providing a forum and applying its own law
- The interest of the interstate judicial system, including location of witnesses and evidence, and which state's law is likely to apply (presumption: a state applies its own law better than any other court would)

## *J. McIntyre Machinery v. Nicastro*

[00:30:00] A UK manufacturer sold industrial machinery through a US distributor. A worker was injured in New Jersey by a McIntyre machine. The plurality (four justices) found no specific jurisdiction in New Jersey.

**Notable features of the plurality opinion:**

### Sovereign-Based Analysis

The plurality frames the question in terms of **sovereignty** rather than defendants' rights. The relevant sovereign for minimum contacts purposes is the specific state asserting jurisdiction — New Jersey — not the United States as a whole. J. The plurality notes that had Congress enacted a long-arm statute authorizing federal courts to exercise jurisdiction based on contacts with the United States as a whole, the constitutional analysis might be different. Under current law (Rule 4(k)(1)(A)), federal courts are tied to state long-arm limits, and those contacts did not extend to New Jersey specifically.

### Revival of Implied Consent Fictions

The plurality seems interested in reviving the implied-consent fiction cast aside after *Pennoyer* — the idea, familiar from *Hess v. Pawloski*, <!-- corrected: "Pawloski" → *Hess v. **The concurrence** (Justice Breyer, joined by Justice Alito) declines to articulate a broader rule and simply applies prior precedent to reach the same result. Lower courts generally treat the concurrence as controlling because it is narrower, but it offers little guidance for future cases.

## *Shaffer v. Heitner*: Does *International Shoe* Apply to Quasi In Rem?

[00:00:00] We resumed where class 7 ended: whether the evolution of in personam jurisdiction doctrine under *International Shoe* and its progeny carries over to quasi in rem.

**The quasi in rem mechanism**: A plaintiff asks a court to seize a piece of property located in the forum that has no relationship to the subject matter of the lawsuit. The purpose is to secure a source of recovery if the plaintiff wins, since the court's power over the seized property is limited to the value of the property.

**Under *Harris v. Balk*** (the old rule): Once the court determined that a debt was physically located in the forum (because the debtor was present), seizing the debt for quasi in rem purposes was permissible regardless of any connection between the property and the litigation.

**In *Shaffer v. Heitner***: The Delaware statute at issue authorized seizure of Delaware stock (held by non-Delaware directors and officers) in essentially any lawsuit, and if the defendant came in to retrieve the seized stock, the defendant would be subject to the court's full in personam jurisdiction — not just quasi in rem jurisdiction limited to the property's value. The Supreme Court was troubled by the statute's breadth.

### Two Readings of *Shaffer*

**Reading 1 — Narrow**: The Court was reacting specifically to the extraordinary breadth of the Delaware statute. Ordinary quasi in rem jurisdiction (where the amount of recovery is genuinely capped at property value) is probably still fine as long as the long-arm statute authorizing it is not as sweeping. When Delaware later amended its statute to limit it to directors-and-officers litigation, that narrower statute was likely valid on this reading.

**Reading 2 — Broad**: *Shaffer* says all assertions of jurisdiction over property must be analyzed under the *International Shoe* framework because a quasi in rem action is really a lawsuit against the **owner** of the property, not against the property itself. If so, the standard specific jurisdiction analysis applies: long-arm statute, minimum contacts linking the defendant, the subject matter, and the forum, and fair-and-reasonable. Most traditional quasi in rem actions would fail this analysis because the whole point of quasi in rem is to sue in a place where the defendant has property but where there are no minimum contacts linking the defendant to the litigation and the forum.

The Court's language in *Shaffer* supports the broad reading, but the result of the case is arguably consistent with the narrow reading. We genuinely do not know which interpretation is correct.

## *Burnham v. Superior Court*: The In-State Tag

[00:25:00] Does physical service on a defendant while present in the forum state — the tag that was the cornerstone of *Pennoyer* — remain sufficient to establish personal jurisdiction after *International Shoe*?

The result in *Burnham* was unanimous (jurisdiction upheld), but the reasoning split the Court exactly 4–4:

- **Four justices** (Scalia plurality) held that the in-state tag is sufficient standing alone, full stop, as it has been historically and as it should remain.
- **Four justices** (Brennan concurrence) held that the in-state tag is *one relevant factor* in the overall *International Shoe* framework — it is relevant to fair-and-reasonable analysis, but it does not automatically suffice.

This means we still do not know whether the tag stands alone or is merely one factor. On any exam or in practice, **argue both sides**.

## Venue: Orientation

[00:00:00] We study **federal court venue** only. State court venue statutes exist but are not covered in this course. Venue is concerned entirely with convenience — the convenient and expeditious resolution of disputes. It has no constitutional dimension and no power dimension. It is governed entirely by statute and determines which **federal district** (not which state) a case may be filed in.

Venue operates like personal jurisdiction in one important respect: if venue is improper and the defendant objects, the district court **must dismiss or transfer** and cannot proceed. Like personal jurisdiction, venue can be **waived** by failing to object promptly.

---

## The Federal Venue Statute: 28 U.S.C. § 1391

**B1**: A plaintiff may lay venue in any district where **any defendant resides**, provided **all defendants reside in the same state**. If even one defendant resides in a different state, B1 is unavailable for any district.

**Residency**:
- For a natural person: **domicile**
- For a corporation and unincorporated association: any district in which the entity is **subject to personal jurisdiction**

**B2**: A plaintiff may lay venue where **a substantial part of the events or omissions giving rise to the claim** occurred, or where the relevant property is located (in rem or quasi in rem proceedings).

**B3**: Available only when neither B1 nor B2 provides any usable district. B3 allows filing wherever any defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction. In practice, B3 is almost exclusively relevant when events occurred entirely outside the United States or when defendants are scattered across multiple states with no overlapping district.

---

## Forum Non-Convenience: When to Use It

[00:15:00] Forum non-convenience is a doctrine that operates by **dismissal** — the court dismisses the case with the expectation that the plaintiff will refile elsewhere.

Use forum non-convenience when:
1. A case is pending in a **state court** and the suggestion is to move it to another state or to a foreign court abroad, or
2. A case is pending in **federal court** and the suggestion is to move it to a **foreign court abroad**

Do **not** use forum non-convenience to move a case between federal courts — that is done through the transfer statutes. Do not use it to move a case from federal court to state court — that cannot be done (absent a forum selection clause directing the case to state court).

### The Forum Non-Convenience Analysis

1. **Presumption**: The court presumes the plaintiff's choice of forum is correct — unless the plaintiff is from abroad, in which case no such presumption applies (per *Piper Aircraft*).
2. **Private interest factors** (*Gulf Oil* factors): Location of witnesses who can be compelled to testify, location of documents and evidence, relative expense of litigation in each location, ability to implead third parties.
3. **Public interest factors** (*Gulf Oil* factors): Court congestion, the relationship between the forum community and the dispute (so that any required jury is drawn from a community with a genuine connection to the case), local interest in having the controversy decided at home.

If the combination of private and public interest factors overwhelms the presumption, the court dismisses.

**The defendant's promise**: In a forum non-convenience dismissal, the defendant may promise not to raise defenses it would otherwise have in the alternative forum — including personal jurisdiction, venue, or statute of limitations objections. This allows the defendant to effectively manufacture a viable forum to which the case can go.

---

## The Transfer Statutes: 28 U.S.C. §§ 1404, 1406, 1631

[00:35:00] The transfer statutes govern movement of cases within the **federal court system**. Do not try to distinguish among them for this course — treat their collective effect as the governing law.

**Key difference from forum non-convenience**: A defendant **cannot** create a transferee court by promising to waive personal jurisdiction and venue defenses there. The transferee court must be a court in which the case "**could have been brought**" — which under *Hoffman v. Blaski* means a **happy court**: one in which the plaintiff could have independently established both personal jurisdiction over the defendants and proper venue as of the time the suit was filed.

### The Four-Question Transfer Analysis

**Question 1: Must I transfer?**
If the current court lacks either personal jurisdiction or venue, the case must be transferred or dismissed. It cannot proceed over the defendant's objection.

**Question 2: May I transfer?**
A transfer is permissible only to a happy court — one where both personal jurisdiction and venue are independently available. If no such court exists, the case must be dismissed (not transferred).

**Question 3: Should I transfer?**
When the present court is a happy court and there is another happy court available, whether to transfer is a forum non-convenience analysis: presume the plaintiff's choice of forum is correct, then weigh the private and public *Gulf Oil* interest factors.

**Question 4: What happens if I do?**

This is the **Greiner Happy Court Rule** — governing horizontal choice of law after a transfer.

Federal courts sitting in diversity use the choice-of-law rules of the state in which they sit (*Klaxon*). After a transfer, the question is which state's choice-of-law rules apply.

- **If the transferor court was a happy court**: The choice-of-law analysis follows the transfer. The transferee court pretends it is sitting in the transferor court's state. Limitations periods and all other substantive law that would have applied under the transferor state's choice-of-law rules continue to apply. The transfer is a change of courtroom, nothing more.

- **If the transferor court was an unhappy court**: The choice-of-law analysis of the transferor court does not follow the transfer. The transferee court applies its own state's choice-of-law rules as if the lawsuit had been filed there from the beginning — including the transferee state's limitations period.

---

## Forum Selection Clauses and *Atlantic Marine*

[00:55:00] A forum selection clause changes the transfer analysis in three ways:

1. **No presumption in favor of plaintiff's choice of forum.** Instead, the court presumes it should transfer to the forum specified in the clause.

2. **Private interest factors are disregarded.** The parties encapsulated all private interest concerns when they negotiated the contract. Only public interest factors are considered to overcome the presumption in favor of transfer.

3. **The Greiner Happy Court Rule does not apply.** If the case is transferred to comply with the forum selection clause, the transferee court's choice-of-law analysis governs — regardless of whether the transferor court was happy or unhappy.

As a practical matter, the combination of these three changes means that a case subject to a forum selection clause will almost always be transferred to the clause's designated forum.

---

## *Stewart v. Ricoh* and Erie <!-- corrected: "Stewart v. Rico" → *Stewart v. Ricoh* -->

*Stewart v. Ricoh* is assigned as an Erie case. When a state law invalidates forum selection clauses and a defendant moves to transfer under a federal transfer statute, which governs? The answer (which we will fully develop in class 10 or 11) is the federal transfer statute, because the statutory law on transfers is valid and applicable federal law under the Erie framework. The rules governing transfer motions — including the effect of forum selection clauses — are part and parcel of the federal statutory transfer scheme.

Note: *Stewart v. Ricoh*'s specific statements about how to handle forum selection clause transfer motions are **not good law**. The controlling authority on that question is *Atlantic Marine*.

## Venue: Basic Doctrine

[00:00:00] Venue is a **convenience doctrine** only. It has nothing to do with constitutional power, nothing to do with a court's authority to hear a particular type of case. It is governed entirely by federal statute and concerns which **federal district** (not which state) a case may be filed in.

Like personal jurisdiction, improper venue requires dismissal or transfer if the defendant objects. Like personal jurisdiction, venue may be waived by failing to object promptly.

### Federal Venue Statute § 1391

- **B1**: Venue is proper in any district where any defendant resides, if all defendants reside in the same state. Residency means domicile for individuals; for corporations and unincorporated associations, any district in which the entity is subject to personal jurisdiction.
- **B2**: Venue is proper where a substantial part of the events or omissions giving rise to the claim occurred, or where relevant property is located.
- **B3**: An outlet provision — available only when neither B1 nor B2 provides any available district. Essentially: if events occurred entirely outside the United States, or if defendants reside in multiple states with no overlapping B1 district and no substantial events in any US district.

## Personal Jurisdiction vs. Venue: How They Differ

[00:15:00] To illustrate with a hypothetical: suppose *Calder* <!-- corrected: "Castro" → *Calder* --> had been filed in the District of New Jersey. There would have been **venue** (the injury occurred there), but no **personal jurisdiction** (the federal court is limited by Rule 4(k)(1)(A) to what the New Jersey state courts could do, and those courts could not exercise personal jurisdiction on those facts).

Conversely, personal jurisdiction can exist without proper venue: if a state allows personal jurisdiction over a defendant, and the plaintiff files in a district within that state where no substantial events occurred, venue under B2 is improper even though personal jurisdiction would be valid.

## Forum Non-Convenience

[00:22:00] Forum non-convenience doctrine applies when:
1. A case is in **state court** and the suggestion is to send it to another state or abroad; or
2. A case is in **federal court** and the suggestion is to send it **abroad**.

It does **not** apply to move a case from one federal court to another federal court — use the transfer statutes for that. It does not apply to move a case from federal court to state court (absent a forum selection clause).

### Standard Analysis

In a garden-variety case without a forum selection clause:
1. **Presume** the plaintiff's choice of forum is correct (except when the plaintiff is from abroad — *Piper Aircraft* holds that presumption does not apply).
2. Consider the **private interest factors** from *Gulf Oil*: ease of access to evidence, compellability of witnesses, practical problems of the litigation.
3. Consider the **public interest factors** from *Gulf Oil*: court congestion, relationship between the community and the litigation, appropriateness of having a local jury sit on the case.
4. If those factors, taken together, overwhelm the presumption, dismiss and invite the plaintiff to refile elsewhere.

**Defendant's option to manufacture an alternative forum**: The defendant may promise not to raise defenses — including personal jurisdiction, venue, and limitations — in the alternative forum, thereby creating a viable forum that the court can recognize in its dismissal order.

**Note**: Forum non-convenience and the transfer statutes differ on this point. Under the transfer statutes, a defendant **cannot** manufacture a happy transferee court by promising to waive defenses there. The transferee court must independently qualify as a place where the plaintiff could have brought the case.

## The Transfer Statutes: §§ 1404, 1406, 1631

[00:35:00] For this course, treat these statutes collectively. The key rule: a case may be transferred only to a court in which it "**could have been brought**" — interpreted in *Hoffman v. Blaski* to mean a **happy court**, one with both personal jurisdiction and proper venue.

### Three Questions

**1. Must I transfer?**
If the court lacks personal jurisdiction or venue over the defendant (and the defendant is objecting), the court must transfer or dismiss.

**2. May I (and should I) transfer?**
May: only to a happy court under *Hoffman*.
Should: a forum non-convenience analysis — presume the plaintiff's choice is correct; weigh private and public *Gulf Oil* factors; transfer if those factors overcome the presumption.

**3. What happens if I do?** — The **Greiner Happy Court Rule**

Because a federal court sitting in diversity uses the choice-of-law rules of its home state (*Klaxon*), a transfer raises the question: which state's choice-of-law rules does the transferee court apply?

- **Transferor court was happy**: The transferee court uses the transferor state's choice-of-law rules, including the transferor state's limitations periods. Limitations periods here are treated as procedural by the forum state — so the transferor forum's limitations period travels with the case. The transfer is just a change of courtroom.
- **Transferor court was unhappy**: The Greiner Happy Court rule does not apply. The transferee court uses its own state's choice-of-law rules. The plaintiff filed in the wrong place and will not benefit from that choice.

### Forum Selection Clauses: *Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court* <!-- corrected: "Atlantic Marine" → *Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court* -->

When a defendant moves to transfer pursuant to a forum selection clause, three things change:

1. The **plaintiff's choice of forum is not presumed correct**. Instead, the court presumes in favor of the forum named in the clause.
2. **Private interest factors are not considered** — they were addressed at the time of contracting. Only public interest factors can overcome the presumption.
3. **The Greiner Happy Court rule does not apply**. The transferee court's choice-of-law rules govern.

The result: cases governed by forum selection clauses almost always transfer to the designated forum.

## Erie: The York Outcome-Determinative Test

[00:00:00] We briefly reviewed the *York* test: ask whether the difference between the federal and state rule is **outcome determinative**. If applying the federal rule rather than the state rule would affect the outcome of the litigation, use state law. If not, use federal law.

As noted, this test almost always points toward state law — nearly anything can be outcome determinative by the time a case is before a court.

---

## Erie: The Hanna Majority Test

[00:05:00] The Hanna majority test has two versions — the **Scalia version** and the **Ginsburg version** — a distinction that will matter when we later read *Shady Grove*.

**Step 1: Identify the federal law at issue and its source.** Is it a constitutional provision, a statute, a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure, or federal common law?

**Step 2: Apply the appropriate sub-test.**

### Constitutional Provisions

Presume validity — the people have spoken. Ask only whether the provision is **applicable**: does it provide an answer to the question at issue? Constitutional provisions are rarely interpreted narrowly; just apply standard constitutional interpretation and follow the provision if it applies.

### Federal Statutes

To measure validity, identify a source of congressional power in the Constitution. For Erie purposes, the relevant powers are typically: the Commerce Clause, the power to create the lower federal courts, the Full Faith and Credit Clause, and the Necessary and Proper Clause augmenting the others. If Congress had the power to enact the statute, it is valid.

Applicability is a standard statutory interpretation exercise. There is slightly more room to interpret a statute narrowly to avoid displacing state law — but not much.

*Example*: In *Stewart v. Ricoh*, <!-- corrected: "Stewart versus Rico" → *Stewart v. Ricoh* --> the transfer statutes were enacted pursuant to Congress's power to create the lower federal courts and the Necessary and Proper Clause, and were therefore valid.

### Federal Rules of Civil Procedure

**Validity** here is a **recitation exercise**, not an analytical one. Recite: the validity of a federal rule is measured by the Rules Enabling Act, which provides that a rule cannot abridge, enlarge, or modify a substantive right. As interpreted in *Sibbach*, <!-- corrected: "Sib Act" → *Sibbach* --> this means asking whether the rule really regulates procedure — does it concern itself with process? It does. Declare the rule valid and move on. Courts are essentially unwilling to strike down a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure on Rules Enabling Act grounds.

**Applicability** is where Scalia and Ginsburg diverge:

- **Scalia's approach**: Apply the federal rule as written without creative interpretation. If the rule applies to the question at hand, use it. If it does not apply, do not use it. Avoid consulting state law at all if the federal rule answers the question.

- **Ginsburg's approach**: Interpret the Federal Rule of Civil Procedure creatively, if possible, to allow the state law policy to receive effect. If the federal rule can be read narrowly enough that it does not answer the specific question at issue, there is no conflict and state law governs.

*Walker* illustrates the Ginsburg structural approach: the Court held that the federal rules' grace period for service did not speak to the question of what *tolls* the statute of limitations. That left room for a prior Supreme Court precedent (rather than state policy) to govern. The key structural move — reading the federal rule as not applicable — is the Ginsburg technique regardless of what it is avoiding.

### Federal Common Law (Judge-Made Law)

When the federal law at issue is federal common law, the valid-and-applicable framework does not apply. Instead, use the **twin aims of Erie** test:

1. Prevent forum shopping — would the use of a federal rule rather than the state rule give plaintiffs' attorneys an incentive to file in federal court?
2. Prevent inequitable administration of the laws.

In practice, this is the outcome-determinative test by another name: if the difference between state and federal law would affect outcomes, attorneys will factor it into forum selection decisions, creating forum shopping incentives. Use the state rule to eliminate the incentive.

### Justice Harlan's Concurrence in *Hanna*

The fourth test defines substantive law as any rule that bears on **primary conduct** — what clients do before they ever file a lawsuit. Harlan does not focus on forum shopping. He focuses on whether the rule at issue affects decisions like where to incorporate or what investments to make.

*Example from *Cohen*: A litigation bond requirement deters frivolous lawsuits, and deterring frivolous lawsuits affects the investment strategies of companies (primary conduct). Therefore it is substantive, and state law should govern. By contrast, the size of a filing paper or the length of a service grace period affects only what lawyers do inside litigation — not primary conduct — so federal law may be used for uniformity.

## Rule 12(b)(6): Motion to Dismiss for Failure to State a Claim

[00:40:00] There are three independent grounds for granting a 12(b)(6) motion:

### Ground 1: Fantastical Facts

The facts alleged are simply too implausible on their face to be credited. Classic example: aliens from Mars land a spaceship and commit some wrong. The complaint is dismissed because no reasonable trier of fact could credit those allegations.

### Ground 2: No Viable Cause of Action Applied to These Facts

The cause of action the plaintiff invokes does not provide a right to recover as applied to the stated facts. Example: a § 1983 claim against fellow students. Section 1983 requires state action, and students are not state actors. No amount of factual development will fix the legal mismatch.

### Ground 3: Insufficient Specificity of Allegations

[00:50:00] The facts are not alleged with sufficient particularity. Two competing frameworks:

**Code pleading**: The plaintiff must plead the underlying facts that, if proved, would establish each element of the claim — not merely the ultimate legal conclusion. A bare allegation of "conspiracy in restraint of trade" or "race discrimination" is insufficient; the complaint must allege the specific conduct that would support that conclusion (statements showing animus, documented patterns of disparate treatment, etc.).

**Notice pleading**: The complaint need not plead the specific underlying facts. It must contain enough information to put the defendant on notice of the nature of the allegations so the defendant can identify where to look for evidence and begin building a defense. In *Gillespie*, even an oddly-worded complaint satisfied this standard because it provided a date, time, party identification, and a basic description of the wrong — enough for defense counsel to begin an investigation and identify the applicable legal theory.

**What comes next**: Modern Supreme Court doctrine — *Twombly* and *Iqbal* — purports to reject notice pleading and instead requires **plausibility pleading**. The argument to be developed next class is that plausibility pleading is functionally just code pleading renamed. The Court introduced a structured two-step analysis, but the underlying demand for specific underlying facts is the same. The Federal Rules were designed to move *away* from code pleading, and the Court is walking that back while avoiding the label.

## Motion to Dismiss: The *Iqbal*/*Twombly* Two-Step

In terms of black letter law for the motion to dismiss, under *Iqbal* and *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly* <!-- corrected: "Bell Atlantic" → Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly --> — but most clearly articulated in *Iqbal* — the Court tells us that we need to engage in a two-step analysis.

In the first step, when reading a complaint, the court should ignore any conclusory statements, any recitation of the elements of the cause of action, and any assertions of an ultimate fact that the jury will have to find — such as negligence for a negligence cause of action, agreement for a Sherman Antitrust Act claim alleging restraint of trade, or discrimination of any kind. We ignore all of those statements. We do not accept them as true.

We then look to what is left over in the complaint: the pleading of the underlying facts that might give rise to an inference of discrimination or an inference of an agreement. We ask whether what remains is sufficient to nudge the complaint across the line from merely possible to plausible, taking into account possible alternative legally innocent explanations.

In *Iqbal*, the Court ignored the complaint's assertion that Ashcroft and Mueller had been acting discriminatorily — or had been deliberately indifferent to the discriminatory conditions of incarceration of Muslim men — and asked what was there in the complaint factually, beyond that assertion, to suggest deliberate indifference or active, animus-based discrimination against Muslim detainees. The Court found the facts insufficient to nudge the complaint from merely possible to plausible, given what it viewed as an innocent alternative explanation: that the conditions of confinement were due to the post-September 11 investigation the FBI was conducting under the Attorney General's leadership.

We saw how the same analysis applied in the *Twombly* context — an allegation of agreement by large telephone companies to restrain trade after the AT&T breakup — and in a simple slip-and-fall case in diversity, sitting in a Virginia federal court. In that case, the basic issue was that under Virginia law, the plaintiff would eventually have to show at trial that the puddle on which she slipped had been there too long, or that the store had known about it in some way. She could not plead the facts she intended to prove because she did not yet know them — she would have to find them in discovery — but she could not reach discovery because she could not plead them in the complaint.

We discussed some of the policy tensions here: how much we will allow complaints to survive motions to dismiss and impose costs on the parties, the court system, and taxpayers; but also the costs of a strict rule — that many meritorious complaints may be dismissed at the motion-to-dismiss stage and never reach discovery.

## Preliminary Injunctions

We then conducted a simulated oral argument on a motion for a preliminary injunction. The four factors courts consider are:

1. Likelihood of success on the merits
2. Irreparable harm
3. Balance of the equities
4. The public interest

In some circuits, these are prerequisites — the moving party must prevail on all four to be entitled to a preliminary injunction. In other circuits, a strong showing on one factor can compensate for a less than fully persuasive showing on another.

### Irreparable Harm

Anything that can be fully compensated by monetary damages after the fact is not irreparable harm. Business loss that can be reasonably quantified does not qualify. Loss of money in some sort of tort does not qualify. Loss of life or limb is irreparable harm, even though we attempt to compensate for it after the fact — money is recognized as inadequate there.

It is also irreparable harm if the loss is monetary but cannot be recovered due to some doctrine like sovereign immunity — for instance, where the government cannot be sued for money damages, or where damages are insufficiently quantifiable. Courts are reluctant to reach that last conclusion, but in some cases they will find that damages would be too hard to quantify after the fact, making the harm irreparable.

### Public Interest

Sometimes there is a statutory scheme in the background — a framework that Congress has enacted. If the plaintiff's request for a preliminary injunction would undermine or run contrary to that statutory scheme, that is a strong argument that granting the injunction would not be in the public interest.

We saw this in the FDA case on the reading. Congress has established a scheme whereby ordinary people cannot access pharmaceuticals unless both the FDA and the prescribing physician agree. The plaintiffs sought a ruling that only the prescribing physician's approval is required. The court held that this was contrary to the statutory scheme, and therefore not in the public interest.

## Notice: The Reasonableness Standard

In terms of black letter law, the Court articulates in *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust* <!-- corrected: "the lane" → Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust --> a general reasonableness standard: efforts to provide notice, in order to be constitutionally sufficient, must be reasonable under all the circumstances.

Several principles help give content to that reasonableness standard:

**First**, in order to judge reasonableness, it often helps to put yourself in the position of someone who actually cared whether the defendant — or whoever is subject to some legal consequence — actually received notice. If you pretended you cared, what would you do?

**Second**, under *Jones v. Flowers*, the duty to act reasonably to provide notice does not stop after the first attempt. If a person or court system attempting to provide notice discovers that the first attempt was unsuccessful, and there is something reasonable that could be done to follow up, that person or institution may be under a continuing duty to try something else.

**Third**, the court system sometimes takes into account changing social and socioeconomic circumstances. For example, courts have generally shifted away from requiring certified mail and have not constitutionalized that requirement. *Jones v. Flowers* suggests that regular mail may sometimes be sufficient — or even preferable — in modern settings. The courts have not generally kept pace with the digitalization of our society and the information age, and we discussed some of the consequences of that.

**Fourth** — and perhaps most important as a black letter rule — actual notice is never the constitutional standard. It is never the case that the Constitution requires actual notice before a party's rights may be extinguished. A court system can extinguish rights, and consider them adjudicated, even if the individual whose rights are being extinguished did not receive actual notice. The standard is reasonableness of the efforts to provide notice.

We worked through specific examples and particular fact patterns, and that is where we left the notice discussion.

---

## Opportunity to Be Heard: The *Mathews v. Eldridge* Test

We then turned to opportunity to be heard — what kind of opportunity must be provided before a consequence is imposed. This covers both pre-judgment actions (such as seizure of property prior to judgment) and actions in the administrative state (such as termination of benefits before a hearing).

The governing black letter law here is the *Mathews v. Eldridge* <!-- corrected: "Matthews versus Eldritch" → Mathews v. Eldridge --> test. The test asks courts to consider three factors in determining whether a procedural scheme meets constitutional minimums for opportunity to be heard:

**Factor One: The private interest** at stake — the interest of all private litigants involved in the adjudicatory proceeding.

**Factor Two: The risk of erroneous determination** under the current scheme, compared to the risk of erroneous determination under the scheme that the challenger proposes. In *Mathews v. Eldridge*, the claimant for SSDI benefits argued that the existing scheme — which allowed benefits to be cut off prior to a live hearing, subject to later reconsideration and appeal — was constitutionally deficient, and that benefits could not be cut off until after a live hearing. The second *Mathews* factor demands a comparison between the world we live in and the alternative world the challenger proposes. What is the difference in risk of erroneous determination between those two schemes?

**Factor Three: The government's interest** — in minimizing the cost and delay of adjudication, and sometimes in avoiding payment of benefits that are not ultimately owed.

### Application in *Mathews v. Eldridge*

The Court distinguished *Mathews v. Eldridge* from *Goldberg v. Kelly* — which involved welfare benefits — on two grounds:

First, on the private interest: the SSDI benefits at issue in *Mathews* did not implicate quite the same magnitude of private interest as welfare benefits. SSDI's purpose is not subsistence — not to allow people to eat, have a roof over their heads, or meet basic human needs. It is instead income maintenance for a period in which a previously employed person becomes unable to work due to disability. It is a form of workers' insurance. Because eligibility is based on work history rather than need or destitution, and because income and assets are irrelevant to eligibility, the Court inferred that subsistence is not the purpose of the scheme.

Second, on the risk of erroneous determination: the type of decision in *Mathews* can generally be made on documents — specifically medical records — whereas the determination in *Goldberg*, according to the Court, requires live testimony. If you are applying this framework to a new fact pattern, focus on the nature of the interest at stake and the kind of decision involved: Can it be made on documents alone, or does it require live testimony? If the latter, it may be more difficult to impose consequences before a live hearing, because the risk of erroneous determination would be too high.

### Other Factors Discussed

We also discussed the risk of erroneous determination that can arise when a clerk — rather than a judge — makes a decision, such as whether to issue a garnishment order or allow seizure of property. Courts have recognized that judges, because they adjudicate more often, are likely to make better decisions. A clerk's involvement might therefore increase the risk of erroneous determination, bearing on the second *Mathews* factor.

We also discussed the difference between schemes that require only conclusory allegations to obtain relief (such as the scheme in *Sniadach* <!-- corrected: "Schneidach" → Sniadach -->, where a clerk could issue a garnishment order upon the filing of a complaint-like document with no evidence required) versus schemes that require the requesting party to submit actual evidence. If evidence is required, that requirement reduces the risk of erroneous determination.

## Property Rights and the First *Mathews* Factor

We emphasized two fact patterns that implicate different aspects of the *Mathews v. Eldridge* test.

The first concerned the significance of property rights on both sides of a dispute. Property rights in an object or piece of real estate can be split: one party may hold legal title (sometimes evidenced by a physical document, such as the title to a house or car) while another party holds possession and is paying off what amounts to a loan. In that situation, both parties have property interests at stake. If one party files an emergency request for repossession, the court may note that the party in possession has a property right — they hold possession of the item and have some equity in it — but so does the filing party, who holds legal title. The existence of property rights on both sides is relevant to Part One of the *Mathews v. Eldridge* test: the private interests at issue.

## Litigation Bonds and Procedural Due Process

We also discussed how bonds work and how they bear on the *Mathews* analysis.

A litigation bond is a promise from an insurance company or a financially solvent third party to pay if the plaintiff is ordered to pay damages due to a wrongful seizure action and is unable to do so. If a scheme requires the plaintiff to post a bond before a court will issue an emergency seizure order, that bond serves two constitutional functions:

**First**, it protects the defendant's private interest by ensuring that if the seizure turns out to be wrongful, the defendant can actually recover damages — even if the plaintiff later becomes insolvent.

**Second**, it reduces the risk of erroneous determination. The insurance company or other bond issuer will factor the plaintiff's likelihood of success on the merits into the price of the bond. If the plaintiff has a strong likelihood of success, the bond price will be low (because the chance the bond will be invoked is small). If the plaintiff's likelihood of success is low, the price will be higher — and if the price is too high, the plaintiff may either be unable to pay it or decide it is not worthwhile. The court can thus treat a plaintiff's inability to obtain a bond as a market signal that the action may lack merit.

That market signal can be distorted, however, by the plaintiff's financial limitations. If a plaintiff simply does not have the resources to obtain a bond regardless of the merits, we have an access-to-justice problem.

## The District Court Opinion's Minimum Factors and When to Use Them

We discussed how useful it would be to have a list of minimum factors we would expect to see in any procedural due process scheme. The district court opinion in *Vitek v. - Adequate notice
- A neutral decision maker
- An opportunity to present evidence
- An opportunity to see the other side's evidence
- A hearing
- A written statement of findings

The plurality would have added the assistance of a lawyer; Justice Powell said help of some kind.

**When are these minimum factors useful versus when is the *Mathews v. Eldridge* test useful?**

The minimum-factors list is particularly useful when constructing a new scheme from scratch — for instance, when a court must address a situation in which no procedural due process is being provided at all, or when a newly created right or benefit scheme springs into existence and we need to design an adjudicatory process for determining eligibility.

The *Mathews v. Eldridge* test is much more useful in adjudicating a case-specific attack upon an existing scheme — when a party has identified an existing scheme and argues it should have some feature it currently lacks. The reason is that *Mathews* demands a comparison between an existing scheme and an alternative. Until you have identified both the existing scheme and the specific alternative the challenger proposes, you cannot really use *Mathews*, because the test requires that comparison. When there is no existing scheme to compare anything to, or when you have not yet defined your proposed alternative, *Mathews* cannot operate. That is what the minimum-factors approach is useful for.

## Overview

This is a summary for Class 15, which focused on the summary judgment motion and illustrations of it. The black letter law comes from Justice Brennan's dissent in *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*. <!-- corrected: "sell attacks" → Celotex Corp. v. Catrett -->

---

## Burdens of Production and Persuasion

Justice Brennan distinguishes two types of burdens:

**The burden of production** is the burden to produce evidence of something. If you fail to produce sufficient evidence, you will typically lose a pending motion — whether you filed it or it was filed against you. What counts as "sufficient" depends on the context, discussed below.

**The burden of persuasion** is the risk of non-persuasion — what happens if the evidence is in perfect equipoise, perfectly equal. If there is no evidence on an issue, or insufficient evidence to tilt the decision maker one way or the other, the evidence is said to be in equipoise, and the party bearing the burden of persuasion on that issue loses.

Burdens of persuasion are assigned by law before litigation starts and they never move. The plaintiff bears the burden of persuasion on each element of each cause of action asserted:

- For negligence: duty, breach, causation, damages
- For breach of contract: existence of a contract, breach, and damages

If the plaintiff fails to meet the burden of persuasion on any element, the plaintiff loses on that claim (though the plaintiff may prevail on other claims). Defendants bear the burden of persuasion on affirmative defenses.

---

## Summary Judgment: The Analytical Framework

The summary judgment record is constructed from discovery materials, which the parties attach to their memoranda in support of or in opposition to the motion. Once the record is complete, the judge applies the analytical framework Justice Brennan articulates.

### When the Moving Party Bears the Burden of Persuasion at Trial

If the moving party bears the burden of persuasion at trial on the issue as to which summary judgment is sought, the moving party must produce evidence that, if unrebutted, shows:

1. There is no genuine issue of material fact; and
2. The moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law — meaning a rational trier of fact could only find that the moving party has met its burden of persuasion by a preponderance of the evidence.

If the moving party fails to meet that burden of production, the judge denies the motion without consulting the other party's evidence. The motion is simply denied.

If the moving party meets that burden, the burden of production shifts to the non-moving party to produce evidence showing there is a genuine issue of material fact requiring a trial. If the non-moving party meets that burden, the judge denies the motion and the case goes to trial. If the non-moving party fails, the judge grants the motion.

### When the Moving Party Does Not Bear the Burden of Persuasion at Trial

If the moving party does not bear the burden of persuasion at trial, Justice Brennan identifies two options:

**Option One: Affirmatively knock out an element.** The moving party produces evidence that affirmatively negates some element of the opposing party's claim or defense. For example, in a negligence case, a defendant might produce evidence that the allegedly negligent act could not have caused the plaintiff's injuries — say, that the substance at issue does not cause the type of cancer the plaintiff suffers from. If the judge concludes the moving party has not produced enough evidence to affirmatively knock out the element (leaving no genuine issue of material fact), the judge denies the motion. If the judge concludes the moving party has done so, the burden of production shifts to the party with the burden of persuasion to demonstrate that a genuine issue of material fact exists.

**Option Two: Show absence of sufficient evidence.** The moving party tries to show that the opposing party has insufficient evidence to meet its burden of persuasion at trial. The example from class was *Jones v. Clinton*: Clinton's lawyers argued there was no evidence of causation between Jones's refusal to engage in sex with President Clinton and the adverse employment consequences she alleged. To show the absence of something, the moving party must take discovery, then present — from the discovery record — whatever the other side is likely to use to meet its burden, and argue why it is insufficient. You cannot simply file a conclusory brief asserting they have no evidence. You must anticipate what they will produce and demonstrate it falls short. If the judge is not satisfied that the moving party has made this showing, the motion is denied immediately. If satisfied, the burden of production again shifts to the non-moving party to come forward with evidence showing a genuine issue of material fact.

---

## "Genuine Issue of Material Fact"

Two important qualifiers:

**Genuine**: it must be an issue that actually requires a trial to resolve — not a manufactured or marginal dispute.

**Material**: the fact must be material to the ultimate disposition of the case. A fact that would be interesting to prove but would not affect the litigation's outcome is not material.

We applied this framework to a range of hypotheticals, and that was the black letter law for the day.

## The Basic Framework

Judges do a great deal of fact-finding in the federal courts. They hear evidence and testimony and conduct much of the business of fact-finding. In order for either side to demand a jury trial, two conditions must both be true:

1. The issue must be on the **law side** of the law/equity distinction, as derived from history; and
2. The issue must be on the **fact side** of the law/fact distinction.

If either condition is not satisfied, there will be no jury trial — the judge will conduct all fact-finding.

---

## The Seventh Amendment and the Law/Equity Distinction

The law/equity distinction applies in civil cases in federal courts. The right to a jury trial under the Seventh Amendment does not apply to the states. The Seventh Amendment "preserves" the right to a jury trial in cases in which that right historically existed, when the amount in controversy exceeds $20 — which is to say, effectively all cases.

In most cases, determining whether a jury trial right attaches is straightforward: look for the historical analog to the claim being asserted, and the answer is usually clear. It is also often easy to tell from the relief requested. In a breach of contract lawsuit, for instance:

- If the plaintiff requests **damages**, there is a right to a jury trial (law side).
- If the plaintiff requests **specific performance** (which is an injunction), there is no right to a jury trial (equity side).

---

## Newly Created Causes of Action: The *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local 391 v. Terry* Two-Part Test

The difficulty arises when Congress creates new causes of action that did not exist in the 1790s, or when Congress creates new decision makers (typically administrative law judges within agencies).

For newly created causes of action, courts apply a two-part test from *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local 391 v. Terry*: <!-- corrected: "Schoffers v. Terry" and "Schauphers v. Terry" → Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local 391 v. Terry -->

**Part One: Find the historical analog.** Look at the cause of action and try to find its best historical analog in terms of the types of parties at issue and their legal relationship. In *Chauffeurs v. Terry*, the Court found a strong analogy between a union member's suit for violation of the duty of fair representation and a historical suit by beneficiaries against a trustee for breach of fiduciary duty.

But the Court also taught us that it is not just the cause of action as a whole that matters — it is the particular issues embedded within the cause of action. In *Chauffeurs v. Terry*, two issues were embedded within the claim:

- **Whether there was a breach of the collective bargaining agreement** — analogous to a garden-variety breach of contract, which was historically on the law side, giving rise to a jury trial right.
- **Whether there was a breach of the duty of fair representation** — analogous to breach of fiduciary duty, which was historically equitable.

The Court found itself in equipoise on the first part — roughly one to one — and so the test depends heavily on Part Two.

**Part Two: The type of remedy requested.** This is, according to the Court, the more important part. Some remedies will be new, with no direct historical analog, in which case we find the closest one. In *Chauffeurs v. Terry*, the back pay the plaintiffs requested was more like damages than restitution: the ill-gotten gains subject to a restitutionary theory were held by the employer, who was not a party to the lawsuit. So the remedy could not be analogized to a restitutionary cause of action, and was instead treated as legal — consistent with a damages award on the law side.

---

## The Public Rights Exception and Administrative Decision Makers

Even when a cause of action would ordinarily carry a right to a jury trial, the absence of that right can be justified by the identity of the decision maker. The **public rights exception** provides that when the government is a party and is acting as a sovereign to enforce public rights — protecting worker safety, enforcing federal regulations, asserting forfeiture rights — adjudication may proceed before an administrative law judge without a jury. This exception is narrow: the government cannot simply be acting as an ordinary economic actor (purchasing goods or services). It must be exercising sovereign functions.

This exception saves much of the modern administrative state, since agencies generally cannot conduct jury trials.

**Note**: Sometimes you must apply both the *Chauffeurs v. Terry* two-part test and the public rights exception. If Congress creates a new cause of action and lodges adjudication in an agency, first determine whether a jury trial right attaches to the cause of action at all. If it does not, the inquiry ends. Only if a jury trial right would otherwise attach do you need to ask whether the public rights exception removes it.

---

## The Law/Fact Distinction

The law/fact distinction often resolves itself through historical practice, precedent, or sheer obviousness:

- Disputes about historical facts — contradictory witness testimony about whether a light was green — go to the jury.
- Statutory interpretation is a legal issue for the judge.

When cases fall near the line and lack historical analog or clear precedent, courts consult **function**: who is the better decision maker?

**Juries** are considered better at:
- Untangling contradictions in testimony and assessing witness credibility
- Representing community standards, given the cross-section of perspectives they bring

**Judges** are considered better at:
- Providing uniformity through precedent, so parties can plan their conduct accordingly
- Construing written instruments, given their training and practice
- Overseeing long-running structural injunction cases

In *Markman v. Westview Instruments*, <!-- corrected: "Markman" reference confirmed --> the Court found no direct historical practice to guide the law/fact distinction for construing the scope of a patent claim. It therefore consulted functional factors and held that claim construction is a matter of law — because judges are better both at providing uniformity and at construing written instruments like patent applications.

## Standards of Review

An appellate court applies one of four standards when reviewing rulings that a trial judge made and that a losing party alleges were incorrect.

### De Novo

*De novo* (Latin: "anew") means the appellate court gives no deference whatsoever to the trial court's decision. It considers the trial court's reasoning the way it would a party's brief — persuasive if well-reasoned, but entitled to no special weight. De novo review applies to the trial court's **rulings on questions of law**.

### Clearly Erroneous and Abuse of Discretion

These two standards are functionally the same. Under either, the appellate court gives the trial judge some discretion and deference, but not unlimited deference. If the appellate court can see what the trial judge did and why, and concludes it was reasonable — even if the appellate court would have decided differently — it will uphold the decision and find no error. If the appellate court concludes the trial judge's decision was far outside the bounds of reasonable judgment, it will find error (though not necessarily reverse, for reasons discussed below).

- **Clearly erroneous**: used to review a trial judge's **findings of fact** when the judge is sitting as the trier of fact (as in an equity lawsuit).
- **Abuse of discretion**: applies to almost everything else — discovery rulings, applications of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (including Rule 59 motions for new trial), evidentiary rulings, and the like. The party must have objected to the ruling or requested what the judge refused to give; if not, only plain error review is available.

### Plain Error

Plain error review applies when a party argues that the trial judge made a mistake the party did not object to, or failed to do something the party did not ask for. The appellate court reviews only for error that is so obviously and egregiously wrong that no rational trial judge would have committed it. Plain error findings are extraordinarily rare.

---

## Harmless Error

Even if an appellate court concludes there was error, it does not automatically reverse or order a new trial. The appellate court must also find that the error was not harmless — meaning there is a reasonable probability the error affected the outcome at trial. If the court concludes the error was harmless, it affirms, even though there was error.

Courts sometimes use harmless error doctrine strategically: rather than deciding whether there was error, the appellate court assumes error arguendo and then concludes that even so, the error would be harmless. In that case, the court can affirm without reaching the merits of the alleged error.

This applies to almost all issues on appeal (with a few exceptions we will not cover in this first-year course).

---

## Jury Instructions

Jury instructions are typically given at the end of evidence and testimony. The judge asks the parties to submit proposed instructions, which are served on each other. The parties may then object to the opposing party's proposals, and the court gives the final instructions.

Key rules:
- If you want a particular jury instruction and do not ask for it, and the judge does not give it, you get only plain error review on appeal.
- If you fail to object to the opposing party's proposed instruction and the judge gives it, any appeal is likewise limited to plain error.

The *Kennedy* case holds that in at least some circuits, if a party proposes an instruction on a reasonably important issue that is close but not quite legally correct, the judge has an obligation to correct it and either give a corrected version or provide another instruction on the same subject. Failure to do so may constitute an abuse of discretion. This is not the rule in all circuits; in some, a judge commits no error by refusing to give a legally incorrect proposed instruction.

---

## Rule 52: Findings of Fact by the Judge

Rule 52 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure requires that when a judge sits as the trier of fact (as in an equity case), the judge must make findings of fact before entering judgment. The findings need not be in writing — they may be stated orally on the record. The judge may also adopt one party's proposed findings of fact and conclusions of law in their entirety.

In *Lansford-Coaldale Joint Water Authority v. Tonolli Corp.* <!-- corrected: "Lance for Caldell" → *Lansford-Coaldale Joint Water Authority v. Tonolli Corp.* --> the Court held that the standard of appellate review does not change when a judge wholesale adopts one party's proposed findings: the clearly erroneous standard applies regardless.

**Reasons for requiring findings of fact:**
1. Forcing the judge to articulate reasons tends to improve the quality of decision-making.
2. It helps the appellate court understand the trial judge's reasoning and exercise meaningful review for clear error.
3. It helps with preclusion doctrine — knowing what was decided and why, which will matter when we study issue preclusion.

---

## Rule 50: Judgment as a Matter of Law

Rule 50 applies when a jury is the trier of fact. It replaced the former directed verdict (pre-verdict) and judgment notwithstanding the verdict, or JNOV (post-verdict), motions.

**Procedure**: A party seeking judgment as a matter of law must:
1. File a **pre-verdict motion** before the jury deliberates. The judge does not rule on it immediately — the judge defers ruling until after the verdict, subject to renewal.
2. **Renew the motion** after the jury returns its verdict.

If the party does not make both the pre-verdict and post-verdict motions, an appellate court will not entertain any argument that the trial court erred in failing to grant judgment as a matter of law.

Why require the pre-verdict motion? To give the opposing party an opportunity to cure any evidentiary gap the motion has identified. Why require the post-verdict renewal? To formally remind the judge that the motion was deferred and still needs a ruling.

**Standard**: The judge makes all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the non-moving party — including resolving credible credibility disputes in the non-moving party's favor — and then asks: could any rational jury find for the non-moving party? If yes, deny the motion. If no rational jury could so find, grant the motion.

"All reasonable inferences" does not mean all conceivable inferences. Under *Scott v. Harris*, if objective evidence — such as a video or indisputably accurate records — clearly refutes a witness's testimony, the judge need not credit that testimony simply because the non-moving party relies on it.

Judgment as a matter of law is a legal determination, so the appellate court reviews a grant or denial de novo.

---

## Rule 59: Motion for New Trial

A Rule 59 motion for new trial may be filed at any time up to 28 days after the trial judge enters judgment.

**Grounds for a new trial:**
1. **Non-harmless trial error**: The judge made a mistake that you can show was not harmless, and you are asking the judge to spare everyone the trouble of an appeal by granting a new trial now.
2. **Verdict against the great weight of the evidence** (from *Yates v. United States* <!-- corrected: "Yates" → Yates v. United States; general principle confirmed -->): Even though the jury's verdict was rational (if it were not, Rule 50 would be the remedy), the judge believes it runs contrary to the great weight of the evidence. One example: the trial judge suspects a witness who testified in favor of the prevailing party was lying. When adjudicating a Rule 50 motion, the judge must assume that testimony is true; when adjudicating a Rule 59 motion for new trial, the judge need not. That is a key distinction between the two motions.

## Rule 6(b): Non-Extendable Time Limits

Rule 6(b) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides that certain time limits specified in the rules — particularly those associated with motions for a new trial and motions for judgment as a matter of law — cannot be extended. Even if all parties agree to an extension, and even if the district court purports to enter an order extending the time, the order has no effect. The reason is a strong policy in favor of finality: the trial should be a final event, and we need finality in the resolution of litigation.

---

## Rule 60(b): Relief from Judgment

Rule 60 is a motion for relief from a prior judgment. The rule's language is broad, suggesting the court system would be quite willing to revisit prior judgments — but in practice, courts are extremely reluctant to do so, and it almost never happens.

Even if a party exhausts all appeals and it later turns out the party was legally correct, the fact that a court previously ruled incorrectly is generally insufficient to reopen the judgment under Rule 60(b). Being right is not enough. The finality policy is very strong. For right or wrong, once litigation is resolved, it stays resolved.

---

## Appellate Jurisdiction: General Principles

We used *Liberty Mutual Insurance Co. v. Wetzel* <!-- corrected: "Liberty Mutual case" → Liberty Mutual Insurance Co. v. Wetzel --> to lay out the black letter law of appellate jurisdiction. Appellate jurisdiction governs when an appeal may be taken.

Two principles parallel subject matter jurisdiction in the district courts:

1. **Appellate courts have an independent duty to notice a potential defect in their own appellate jurisdiction**, even if neither party raises it. If such a defect is found, the court must immediately dismiss the appeal and return the case to the district court. The parties cannot by agreement create appellate jurisdiction.
2. **Only Congress can grant appellate jurisdiction.** Appellate courts have no jurisdiction without a congressional grant. Congress controls the appellate courts through the appellate jurisdiction statutes just as it controls district courts through the subject matter jurisdiction statutes.

There is also a strong policy in the federal system against **interlocutory appeals** — appeals filed before the district court's final judgment. If a party believes the district court made a mistake (in denying a motion to dismiss, in a discovery ruling, in an evidentiary ruling), the party must generally wait until the final judgment and then appeal everything at once. The policy reflects a judgment that most district court decisions are correct, most errors will be corrected before final judgment, and most that are not will be harmless in any event.

---

## 28 U.S.C. § 1291: The Final Judgment Rule

The workhorse appellate jurisdiction statute is 28 U.S.C. § 1291, which grants the circuit courts appellate jurisdiction over **final decisions** of the district courts. "Final decision" means the district court's final judgment — the definitive resolution of the case. Everything up to that point must ordinarily await that final judgment before it can be appealed.

---

## Rule 54(b): Partial Final Judgment

Rule 54(b) is not an independent basis for appellate jurisdiction. It is an interpretation of what "final judgment" means under § 1291, available only when:

1. The case involves **more than one claim**. For Rule 54(b) purposes, "claim" means a legal theory + a set of facts + a party. Any variation in one of those three components creates a different claim. (*Liberty Mutual* involved only one claim and therefore could not use Rule 54(b).)
2. The district court has **finally decided some but not all claims**.
3. The district court **expressly finds**, either orally on the record or in writing, that there is **no just reason for delay**, and enters judgment on those adjudicated claims.

When these conditions are met, the losing party on the adjudicated claims may take an immediate appeal. A 30-day clock starts running from the entry of judgment; failure to file a notice of appeal within 30 days forfeits the right to appeal.

The key consideration in finding "no just reason for delay" is whether the adjudicated claims are sufficiently unrelated — factually, legally, and logically — from the remaining claims that the appellate court can hear the appeal now without inefficiency, rather than being forced to revisit the same subject matter again when the remaining claims are later decided.

---

## The Cohen Exception (Collateral Order Doctrine)

The *Cohen* exception is another interpretation of "final decision" under § 1291. Appellate jurisdiction still derives from § 1291, but *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.* <!-- corrected: "Cohen exception" origin case → Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp. --> defines a category of orders that are "final" for purposes of § 1291 even though the case as a whole is not over.

Three requirements must be met (with a possible fourth):

1. **Finality as to the issue**: The district court's ruling on that particular issue is final — the district court does not anticipate revisiting it. (A summary judgment ruling is not final in this sense because Rule 56 itself says such orders can be reconsidered. But most orders satisfy this criterion.)
2. **Collateral to the merits**: The issue must not be intertwined with the merits of the case. We do not want an appellate court resolving issues closely tied to the merits while merits litigation continues in the district court.
3. **Effectively unreviewable later**: A later appeal at the end of the case can provide no practical remedy. This is the hardest criterion to meet.
4. **(Stealth criterion)**: Courts are reluctant to apply the *Cohen* exception to highly discretionary rulings that require extensive familiarity with the record. They prefer to apply it to clean, binary legal issues — up or down, this way or that.

**Examples from the cases:**

*In Cohen itself*: Whether a plaintiff in a shareholder derivative action had to post a litigation bond was immediately appealable. If the district court ruled (as an Erie matter) that no bond was required, there was no effective way to challenge that ruling after the case ended — by then, no insurer would sell the plaintiff a bond at a reasonable price.

*Not effectively unreviewable (no Cohen exception)*:
- A district court order to disclose allegedly privileged material — reviewable after final judgment, when the court can assess whether the material was used at trial and whether any error was harmless.
- A forum selection clause issue — if the clause should have been enforced, the appellate court can simply direct dismissal and invite the parties to relitigate at the proper forum.

*Cohen exception present*: A *Colorado River* stay order (discussed in the prior class period on Colorado River abstention).

---

## 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1): Injunction Appeals

28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1) provides appellate jurisdiction over district court orders:

- Granting or refusing a preliminary injunction
- Modifying or refusing to modify an injunction
- Dissolving or refusing to dissolve an injunction

The rationale: either the enjoined party is suffering adverse consequences and needs appellate review, or an emergency has been alleged and the district court has declined to act. In either case, we want the appellate court to be able to take a look.

**Important limitation**: A district court's refusal to *rule* on a motion for an injunction is not immediately appealable under § 1292(a)(1). If the district court is simply sitting on the motion, your only possible avenue is to petition the appellate court for a writ of mandamus ordering the district court to issue a ruling. That is strategically dangerous — it tends to annoy the district court — and will be addressed in the next class.

## 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b): The "Double Discretion" Doctrine

28 U.S.C. § 1292(b) allows a district court to certify an important but unresolved legal issue to the court of appeals before final judgment. It is called the "double discretion" doctrine because two actors must each exercise discretion in the party's favor.

For certification, the district court must state **in writing** that:

1. The order involves a **controlling question of law** (an important question material to the case);
2. There is a **substantial ground for difference of opinion** on that question — it cannot be an obvious or settled issue; and
3. An immediate appeal would **materially advance the ultimate termination of the litigation**.

Once the district court makes those findings in writing, the party seeking the appeal has **10 days** to petition the court of appeals for permission to appeal. The court of appeals has essentially unbridled discretion to accept or deny the petition.

Hence "double discretion": the district court's willingness to certify, and the circuit court's willingness to accept.

---

## Petitions for Writ of Mandamus

Another way to get a district court ruling before the circuit court is to petition for a writ of mandamus. Courts are generally reluctant to grant these petitions, in part because of how the proceeding begins: the petitioner files a new lawsuit in the circuit court, with the district judge as the nominal respondent. The district judge must then either defend the ruling personally or rely on the opposing party to do so (which the opposing party may or may not do).

The authorizing statute is the All Writs Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1651, which simply authorizes courts to issue writs. The circumstances under which mandamus may issue are governed by historical practice and developed doctrine.

**Two categories of mandamus:**

**Garden-variety writ**: The petitioning party must show:
1. The right to relief is **clear and indisputable** — the circuit court must be quite confident the petitioner is correct on the merits (a high bar, since presumably the district court disagreed);
2. There is some **exigent circumstance** — a reason to act now rather than waiting for final judgment; and
3. Issuing the writ would be in **equity and good conscience and in accordance with sound judicial administration**.

These factors are functionally similar to the four preliminary injunction factors from Rule 65, though Rule 65 does not apply in the court of appeals.

**Supervisory writ**: When a district court has been doing the same problematic thing repeatedly, the circuit court may issue a supervisory writ of mandamus to correct the pattern. The circuit court does not label the writ as "supervisory" — it simply issues the writ — but the supervisory nature of the situation is an argument the petitioner can make to the circuit court in support of the petition.

---

## Joinder: Adding Parties and Claims Beyond the Basic Case

A single plaintiff suing a single defendant on a single theory of relief requires no special authorization under the Federal Rules. Any additional complexity requires both:

1. **Authorization under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure** (permissive or compulsory joinder rules); and
2. **Subject matter jurisdiction** over each new claim or party.

The analysis is a two-step inquiry for each new claim or party:
- Step 1: Is it permitted under the applicable federal rule?
- Step 2: Is there subject matter jurisdiction?

For subject matter jurisdiction: first check whether the claim arises under federal law (either because Congress created the cause of action, or under the *Smith v. Kansas City Title* <!-- corrected: "Smith-Grabel" → Smith v. Kansas City Title and Grable & Sons Metal Products v. Darue Engineering --> /*Grable* framework). If not, check for diversity jurisdiction (complete diversity + amount in controversy over $75,000). If neither applies, check supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367, but be careful about claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rule 14, 19, 20, or 24 when the anchoring claim is a diversity case.

**The transaction or occurrence test** is central to many joinder rules:
- **Compulsory counterclaims** (Rule 13(a)): must be asserted if they arise from the same transaction or occurrence
- **Permissive counterclaims** (Rule 13(b)): may be asserted even if from a different transaction or occurrence
- **Cross-claims** (Rule 13(g)): allowed when they arise from the same transaction or occurrence
- **Impleader of a third-party defendant** (Rule 14): an exception — the test is not transaction or occurrence but rather whether the original defendant seeks to shift liability to the third party

Courts tend to define "same transaction or occurrence" broadly, asking whether the actions are similar in time, space, legal issues, and evidentiary overlap. The policy favors efficiency: litigating related claims together is more efficient than separate lawsuits. If the resulting case becomes too unwieldy, Rule 42 allows the district court to sever claims, control the order of trial, and otherwise manage the proceeding.

---

## Intervention

Two types of intervention exist — intervention as of right and permissive intervention — but you are not responsible for distinguishing between them. The court of appeals in *Smuck v. Hobson* <!-- corrected: "SMAC" → *Smuck v. Hobson* --> did not think much of the distinction either.

When a non-party seeks to intervene in a lawsuit, courts consider:

1. Does the proposed intervenor have a **legally cognizable, protectable interest** at stake in the litigation?
2. Is there already a party in the lawsuit **adequately protecting** that interest?
3. Would adding the new party make the lawsuit **too unwieldy to administer**?

- No cognizable interest → no intervention
- Interest already adequately represented → no intervention
- Addition would make the case unmanageable → no intervention

## The Elements of Claim Preclusion

Claim preclusion has **three stated elements**, plus two that are usually unstated:

**Stated elements:**
1. Same party
2. Same claim
3. A judgment that is final and on the merits

**Unstated ("stealth") elements:**
4. The rendering court in Lawsuit 1 has not explicitly limited the claim-preclusive effect of its own judgment
5. The rendering court in Lawsuit 1 had jurisdiction over the theory of relief to be precluded in Lawsuit 2

---

## Policy: Why Claim Preclusion Exists

Claim preclusion requires a Lawsuit 1 that has gone to final judgment, and then a Lawsuit 2 in which a party that was — or should have been — in the posture of asserting theories of relief in Lawsuit 1 is now asserting or withholding them. The doctrine's purpose is efficiency: we want parties to bring all related theories of relief in one lawsuit. Claim preclusion achieves this by extinguishing theories of relief that were not brought in Lawsuit 1, even if they were never actually asserted. The threat of extinguishment is the incentive to consolidate.

This distinguishes claim preclusion from issue preclusion, which covers only things actually litigated and decided. Claim preclusion does not care whether the theory was asserted or not — typically it applies precisely because it was not.

Another key distinction: claim preclusion is indifferent to who won. It applies equally whether the plaintiff or the defendant prevailed in Lawsuit 1. (Issue preclusion, by contrast, does care who won.)

---

## Element 1: Final Judgment on the Merits

**Finality** for claim preclusion purposes is met once the trial court enters a final judgment, even if the case is still on direct appeal. A party in Lawsuit 2 may plead claim preclusion as soon as Lawsuit 1 reaches final judgment in the trial court, provided the other elements are met.

**"On the merits"** — some examples:

*Clearly not on the merits (no claim preclusion):*
- Dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction, personal jurisdiction, or venue

*Clearly on the merits (claim preclusion applies):*
- Cases decided at trial
- Cases resolved by summary judgment followed by final judgment
- Cases dismissed for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6) followed by final judgment

*Harder cases:*
- **Dismissal for failure to prosecute, or as a discovery sanction**: typically treated as on the merits as a policy matter, to deter repetitive litigation.
- **Dismissal based on the statute of limitations**: more complex. Whether a limitations-based dismissal has claim-preclusive effect can vary by jurisdiction.

Example: A buyer of a defective truck has two theories — a products liability tort claim (short limitations period) and a breach of written contract claim (longer limitations period). If the buyer sues only on the tort after the limitations period has run and the case is dismissed, does that dismissal preclude the buyer from later suing for breach of the written contract, which is still timely? States disagree on this, and the federal system has its own view, which we will address next week.

---

## Element 2: Same Party

The general rule is that "same party" means the same legal entity. Spouses are not the same party. Business partners are not the same party. Corporations that hold interests in each other are not the same legal entity — absent a special relationship.

That special relationship is **privity**. For purposes of this course, parties are in privity in two circumstances only:

1. **Substantial control**: The party in Lawsuit 2 exercised substantial control over the conduct of the party in Lawsuit 1. Example: a sole shareholder of a corporation, where the corporation was the plaintiff in Lawsuit 1 and the shareholder is the plaintiff in Lawsuit 2.

2. **Successor in interest**: The party in Lawsuit 2 is a successor in interest to the party in Lawsuit 1. Example: a party sells the right to collect a debt to another party after filing a lawsuit on that debt. The successor, having acquired the interest, is bound by the prior judgment.

If neither of these applies, the parties are legally distinct and the ordinary rule governs — different legal entities are different parties.

---

## Element 3: Same Claim

"Claim" for claim preclusion purposes means **transaction or occurrence** — not a legal theory. This is the same broad transaction-or-occurrence test we encountered in *Heyward-Robinson* <!-- corrected: "Hayward Robinson" → Heyward-Robinson --> in the context of joinder. It is a deliberately broad and encompassing concept.

Ask: Are the two theories of relief related in time, space, and logic? Is the evidence needed to prove each similar? Would there be efficiency gains from requiring a plaintiff to bring both together? If yes, they are part of the same claim for preclusion purposes — regardless of whether they rest on different legal theories.

The court in *Gonzalez v. Banco Central Corp.* <!-- corrected: "Gonzalez" → Gonzalez v. Banco Central Corp.; retained as is --> states the law correctly. The dictum in that case says the same claim rule applies to the individual plots of land that were subject to the overall fraud, and each plot would ordinarily be the same transaction or occurrence. But the instructor finds the application troubling, because treating all plots as one "claim" would undermine the ability to sell individual parcels separately — which brings us to the next point.

### Exceptions Based on Market Structure

Sometimes the policy consequences of treating two things as the same claim are intolerable. In those cases, courts carve out exceptions by definition. The main example:

When we want to maintain a **functioning market** in individually tradeable items, we treat each item as a separate claim. Even if 2,000 bonds were sold in a single transaction, we define each bond — and each coupon on each bond — as a separate claim, so they can be separated and sold individually. The same logic applies when we want real property parcels to be individually alienable.

### Installment Contracts

For installment contracts (contracts with repeated payments over time):
- All **past-due and unpaid** payment obligations, as of the time the lawsuit is filed, are part of the same claim — we want one lawsuit for all past-due amounts.
- **Future payments** not yet owed are not part of the same claim — we do not yet know whether they will be missed.

---

## The "Stealth" Elements

### Limitation by the Rendering Court

A court in Lawsuit 1 may reduce the claim-preclusive effect of its own judgment by expressly dismissing a claim **"without prejudice."** A court may not, however, increase the claim-preclusive effect beyond what the law would otherwise provide simply by using the phrase "with prejudice." Use of that phrase means a subsequent court will examine the circumstances to determine whether claim preclusion is warranted.

### Jurisdiction of the Rendering Court: *Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons* <!-- corrected: "Marese" and "Roland Maresen" → Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons -->

In order for claim preclusion to extinguish a particular theory of relief in Lawsuit 2, the rendering court in Lawsuit 1 must have had the legal **ability** to adjudicate that theory. In *Marrese*, a state court was the rendering court in Lawsuit 1. The plaintiffs had sued on state antitrust causes of action. The state court could not hear federal antitrust claims because Congress had made federal antitrust jurisdiction exclusive to the federal courts — not because the state court was unwilling, but because it lacked authority. When the plaintiffs later brought federal antitrust claims in federal court based on the same facts, they were not claim precluded, even though they were the same parties, the same transaction or occurrence was involved, and the state court judgment was final on the merits. The rendering court simply could not have adjudicated the federal antitrust claim.

## Overview

Class 21 focused mostly on issue preclusion and began introducing the challenges of inter-system preclusion.

## Elements of Issue Preclusion

Issue preclusion has three essential elements: same issue, necessary to the decision, and actually litigated. The elements "same issue" and "actually litigated" will sometimes tend to merge a little, which is fine, because we need all three elements in order for issue preclusion to run. There are also a few stealth elements, just as there are with claim preclusion.

With those elements in mind, issue preclusion and claim preclusion are fairly different doctrines:

- In **claim preclusion**, we do not care who won, and we preclude theories of relief whether or not they were actually raised in lawsuit one.
- In **issue preclusion**, we only preclude losers — people who lost in litigation one — and we only preclude things that were actually litigated.

Walk through the elements of issue preclusion to decide whether it applies; do not mix and match the two doctrines.

### Same Issue

As illustrated in *Cromwell v. County of Sacramento*, <!-- corrected: "Cromwell versus County of SAC" → Cromwell v. County of Sacramento --> issues are fairly narrow and they are factual. Issue preclusion does not preclude relitigation of purely legal issues — for that, we have stare decisis. Issue preclusion applies only to factual issues, and they must be fairly narrow. Not "can somebody recover in this lawsuit" or "are all the elements of this cause of action the same as in litigation one," but rather something like whether a party is a holder in due course — which is what was at issue in *Cromwell* (did the party buy the bonds without knowledge of the fraud prior to the maturity date?). Or: was there fraud in a particular transaction? Were statements made in the context of a transaction fraudulent or misleading? Was a proxy statement materially misleading? Those are the sorts of issues for which we use issue preclusion.

### Actually Litigated

We only use issue preclusion for something that the parties did raise and fight about. There is no rule requiring parties to raise everything they could have raised. That means, for example, that even though in *Cromwell v. County of Sacramento* it was arguably Cromwell's fault that he did not raise the question of holder-in-due-course status, we still allow him to litigate it for the first time in lawsuit two. We think the incentives to raise issues in lawsuit one are sufficient on their own — you want to win lawsuit one — so we do not need the additional incentive of precluding unraised issues in lawsuit two.

### Necessary to the Decision

The fact pattern of *Rios v. Davis* <!-- corrected: "Rios versus Davis" → Rios v. Davis --> illustrates what "necessary to the decision" means: the finding must matter for the judgment the court issues. If a finding has no effect on the judgment, then it is not necessary to the decision.

In *Rios v. Davis*, the finding of negligence against Rios was not necessary to the court's decision that Davis recover nothing from Rios, because once Davis is found negligent, the inquiry ends — it does not matter whether Rios was also negligent.

A more challenging scenario arises when there are two independent grounds for a jury verdict and the jury returns a general verdict that does not distinguish between them:

- Some jurisdictions (including the rule articulated by the U.S. Supreme Court in *Russell v. Place*) hold that because we do not know which of those grounds the general verdict rested on, we cannot say that any one of them was necessary to the decision, and therefore issue preclusion does not apply.
- Other jurisdictions take the opposite view: if there are multiple independent grounds for a general jury verdict, they will assume the jury ruled in favor of the prevailing party on all of them, and thus treat each as necessary to the decision.

## Mutuality

Mutuality may or may not be required, and if required it may have different meanings. There are four views:

1. **No mutuality required (New York view):** Issue preclusion runs regardless of whether there is mutuality.

2. **Strict mutuality:** Same parties in lawsuit one and lawsuit two, and they must be in the same procedural posture — same plaintiff, same defendant.

3. **Same parties, different posture:** The same two parties must have participated in the first lawsuit in an adversarial posture, but they need not be in the same procedural posture. A party that was a defendant in lawsuit one may become a plaintiff in lawsuit two, and vice versa.

4. **Federal common law view (*Parklane Hosiery*):** <!-- corrected: "Park Lane hosiery" → Parklane Hosiery -->
   - **Defensive non-mutual collateral estoppel** — where the plaintiff is the same in both lawsuits and lost on an issue against defendant one, and defendant two seeks to use that loss to preclude the plaintiff from relitigating that issue — is generally **allowed** under federal common law.
   - **Offensive non-mutual collateral estoppel** — where the defendant is common to both lawsuits and a second plaintiff wants to use the defendant's loss in lawsuit one to prevent the defendant from relitigating that issue — is generally **not allowed** under federal common law.

The reason for the distinction is incentive structure:

- Allowing defensive non-mutual collateral estoppel incentivizes the common plaintiff to sue all defendants at once, since losing against one will be used by others.
- Allowing offensive non-mutual collateral estoppel would incentivize a second plaintiff to sit out of lawsuit one — if plaintiff one wins, plaintiff two can use the ruling offensively; if plaintiff one loses, plaintiff two is still not precluded because preclusion cannot run against a party who has not had their day in court. That wait-and-see incentive is bad.

However, the Supreme Court in *Parklane Hosiery* noted that this reasoning depends on plaintiff two having been able to join the first lawsuit. If there was a legal impediment to joining, the rationale for disallowing offensive non-mutual collateral estoppel disappears, and we might as well allow it to save the costs of relitigating issues already decided. In *Parklane Hosiery* itself, that exception applied and the Court allowed offensive non-mutual collateral estoppel.

## General Rules Applicable in All Issue Preclusion Situations

- We do not preclude parties who have not had their day in court — each party is entitled to one day in court.
- The concept of privity from claim preclusion applies to issue preclusion as well: preclusion runs against parties or their privies.
- Issue preclusion generally does not run when the party to be precluded in lawsuit two did not have a full and fair opportunity to litigate in lawsuit one. This might occur because of unusual discovery rules severely limiting discovery, inability to obtain witness testimony, or lack of access to documents.
- Issue preclusion can sometimes operate in unexpected ways: different sources of law may require litigation of the same facts. Even if the source of law in lawsuit one differs from the source of law in lawsuit two, if there are factual issues in common that are truly the same, we may allow issue preclusion to run in lawsuit two. The facts of *Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons* <!-- corrected: "Marese" → Marrese --> illustrate this.

## Introduction to Inter-System Preclusion

We began to unpack the general situation in which the rendering court is from one jurisdiction and the subsequent court is from another. We identified at least four scenarios:

| Rendering Court | Subsequent Court |
|---|---|
| State | State |
| State | Federal |
| Federal | State |
| Federal | Federal |

The sources of governing law:

- **Article IV, § 1 of the Constitution** (Full Faith and Credit Clause) speaks only to the first scenario: state rendering court, state subsequent court.
- **28 U.S.C. § 1738** speaks to the first two scenarios: state rendering court followed by state subsequent court, and state rendering court followed by federal subsequent court.
- When the **rendering court is federal** — whether the subsequent court is state or federal — neither the Constitution nor § 1738 applies by their plain terms. Despite Rule 41 <!-- corrected: "Rule 51" → Rule 41 --> purporting to apply, the better view is that it is not a rule of preclusion.

Because leaving federal court judgments without any preclusive framework would be disastrous — parties would be free to relitigate anything decided by a federal court — we need **federal common law** to fill the gap. Federal common law will insist on full faith and credit for federal court judgments in both subsequent federal courts and subsequent state courts.

What remains to be unpacked is: (1) what does full faith and credit mean in this context, and (2) what is the content of the federal common law, and will it vary according to the basis of subject-matter jurisdiction in lawsuit one? The answer is that it will vary, and that will be addressed in the next class.

## Overview

Class 22 dealt primarily with inter-system preclusion. A portion of the class also addressed class actions; the black-letter law on class actions is covered in the summary for Class 23.

## Inter-System Preclusion: Four Scenarios

There are at least four scenarios to care about when the rendering and subsequent adjudicators are courts:

| Rendering Court | Subsequent Court | Governing Law |
|---|---|---|
| State | State | Article IV, § 1 of the Constitution + 28 U.S.C. § 1738 |
| State | Federal | 28 U.S.C. § 1738 |
| Federal | State | Federal common law |
| Federal | Federal | Federal common law |

## The Full Faith and Credit Obligation

Under all of those sources of law — Article IV, § 1, § 1738, and federal common law — the obligation of the subsequent court is to give **full faith and credit** to the decision of the rendering court. That means the subsequent court must give at least as much preclusive effect to the rendering court's judgment as the rendering court's own system would give.

- If the rendering court system would preclude (claim preclusion or issue preclusion), the subsequent court **must** also preclude.
- If the rendering court system would not preclude, the subsequent court may have some discretion — it might choose to preclude anyway for reasons of judicial economy, or it might not.

## When the Rendering Court Is a State Court

The straightforward application, illustrated in *Migra v. Warren City School District Board of Education*, <!-- corrected: "Negroes" → *Migra v. Warren City School District Board of Education* --> is that where the rendering court was a state court and the subsequent court is a federal court, § 1738 governs and the Supreme Court says you consult the law of the state to determine the preclusive effect of the state court judgment in federal court.

## When the Rendering Court Is a Federal Court

### Arising-Under Jurisdiction

When the rendering federal court is exercising arising-under (federal-question) jurisdiction, and the subsequent court is also a federal court exercising arising-under jurisdiction, cases like *Parklane Hosiery* <!-- corrected: "Park Lane hosiery" → Parklane Hosiery --> establish that the Supreme Court does not consult state law. The federal common law rule is **uniform** regardless of the geographic location of the district courts involved, and that uniformity holds regardless of the nature of the subsequent court.

### Diversity Jurisdiction

When the rendering court is a federal court sitting in **diversity**, the content of the federal common law full faith and credit rule is different. In *Semtek International Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.* <!-- corrected: "SimTech" and "SymTech" and "CEMTECH" → Semtek --> the Supreme Court held that a subsequent state court must give a diversity federal court's judgment the preclusive effect that would be given by the courts of the state in which the rendering federal court sits.

In *Semtek* itself, the rendering court was a federal district court in California sitting in diversity. Therefore, the subsequent state court (in Maryland) had to give as much preclusive effect as a California state court would have given in the same circumstances — California state law governed.

The reason, as the *Semtek* Court explains, is essentially Erie concerns or Erie policies, even though the Erie tests are not directly applied: we want uniformity between the result that would have obtained had the rendering court been a California state court and the result when the rendering court is a California federal court sitting in diversity.

The prevailing view in the civil procedure scholarly community, and the rule to use for this class, is that the *Semtek* principle extends regardless of the identity of the subsequent court or its basis of jurisdiction. Any time a rendering court is a federal court sitting in diversity, the preclusive effect of that judgment is determined by the law of the state in which it sits.

## Open Questions

Several situations are not clearly resolved:

1. **Dual-basis jurisdiction:** What if both diversity and arising-under jurisdiction are present?
2. **Supplemental claims:** What if there is an arising-under anchoring claim with supplemental state-law claims involved?
3. **Transferred cases in diversity:** When the sole basis of jurisdiction is diversity and the case has been transferred, do we use the transferor state's preclusion law or the transferee state's preclusion law?

On an exam, if one of these unresolved questions is presented, argue the policy arguments on both sides: policy arguments for applying a uniform federal common law rule regardless of geography, and policy arguments for applying the preclusion law of the state in which the rendering federal district court sits.

**Important reminder:** The whole inter-system preclusion analysis is only relevant when the rendering court's preclusion law and the subsequent court's preclusion law actually differ. If they are the same, there is nothing to worry about.

## Ways Preclusion Law Can Differ Among Jurisdictions

We have already encountered at least three:

1. Whether a statute-of-limitations dismissal is on the merits for claim preclusion purposes.
2. Whether a general jury verdict with multiple independent possible bases means all those bases are considered decided in favor of the prevailing party (the *Russell v. Place* question).
3. Whether mutuality is required at all — New York generally requires none; some jurisdictions require strict mutuality (same parties, same procedural posture); others require same parties but allow different procedural postures; and under federal common law (*Parklane Hosiery*) there is a distinction between offensive and defensive non-mutual collateral estoppel.

## Agency Adjudications

When the rendering adjudicatory body is an agency rather than a court, federal common law governs (there is no statute). The Restatement (Second) of Judgments provides the general common law rule: a subsequent court (or agency) will ordinarily afford preclusive effect — both issue-preclusive and claim-preclusive — to an adjudicatory judgment from an agency, but only if that agency proceeding complies with minimum due process requirements.

The Restatement identifies some minimal requirements:

- Notice to the relevant parties
- An opportunity to present evidence and arguments
- A neutral decision-maker
- A clear rule of finality articulating when the decision is final

Things that are **not** required for minimum due process in this context include: court-appointed or lawyer assistance, a right of appeal, discovery, and a live hearing (paper submissions suffice).

This minimal due process standard is even less demanding than that articulated in *Vitek v. Jones*, <!-- corrected: "VTEC" → Vitek v. Jones --> which is itself less demanding than the procedural protections available in federal court.

The Restatement notes that its list is not exhaustive: in some circumstances, we might require more before affording preclusive effect to an agency determination. To assess what additional process might be required, we would consider the *Mathews v. Eldridge* <!-- corrected: "Matthews versus Eldridge" → Mathews v. Eldridge --> factors:

- The importance of the private interest at stake (the higher the stakes, the more process we want)
- The ease and cost of adding process
- The reduction in risk of erroneous determination that additional process would provide
- The government's interest in minimizing cost

## Limits on Agency Preclusion: Scope of Agency Authority

A critical warning: agency adjudicators frequently have very limited decision-making authority, confined to particular narrow issues or claims. For example, a Social Security Administration ALJ (administrative law judge) may only determine whether a claimant is disabled — not whether the agency's own processes are constitutional or whether its decisions reflect a pattern of discriminatory decision-making.

Because of the rule of *Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons*, <!-- corrected: "MADESE" → Marrese --> things the agency adjudicator lacks the power to adjudicate are not claim-precluded in a subsequent lawsuit by a prior agency adjudication. There was no opportunity to litigate those matters before the agency, so preclusion cannot run as to them.

## Overview

Class 23 focused almost entirely on class actions, and also covered the rule that emerges from *Beacon Theaters v. Westover*. <!-- corrected: "Beacon Theaters" — full case name is Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover; flagged for review -->

## Subject Matter Jurisdiction in Class Actions

### Arising-Under Claims

If a class action is filed in federal court and all class members assert arising-under (federal-question) causes of action — as was the case in *Parklane Hosiery*, <!-- corrected: "Park Lane Hosiery" → Parklane Hosiery --> a private class action where each member asserted claims under federal securities law — there is no subject-matter jurisdiction problem. 28 U.S.C. § 1331 covers each unnamed class member and the named plaintiff alike.

### Diversity Jurisdiction in Class Actions

If the only possible basis for jurisdiction is 28 U.S.C. § 1332, you need to work through both the complete-diversity requirement and the amount-in-controversy requirement in the class action context.

**Complete diversity:** Under *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble* (a pre-Rule 23 case incorporated into Rule 23 jurisprudence), <!-- corrected: "Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur" — flagged; full cite is Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356 (1921) --> you look to diversity only of the named plaintiff(s) and the defendant. Unnamed class members are not considered.

**Amount in controversy:** As long as one named plaintiff is completely diverse and satisfies the amount-in-controversy requirement, the supplemental jurisdiction statute — 28 U.S.C. § 1367 — can be used to assert supplemental jurisdiction over unnamed class members (and even other named plaintiffs) who do not meet the amount-in-controversy requirement. This is the holding of *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc.* <!-- corrected: "Exxon" → Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc. --> That approach works only if the class action is against a **single defendant**; it does not work against multiple defendants.

### The Exxon Rationale

The reasoning in *Exxon Mobil v. Allapattah* applies equally to simple joinder of plaintiffs suing a single defendant and to class actions. The Court's logic:

1. As long as there is an anchoring claim — a diversity claim over which there is original jurisdiction under § 1332 (a completely diverse named plaintiff who meets the amount in controversy) — § 1367(a) authorizes jurisdiction to the constitutional limit.
2. The presence of amount-in-controversy (AIC) deficient claims does not contaminate the anchoring claim, because those claims do not reduce the importance of the $75,000+ anchoring claim.
3. By contrast, the presence of **non-diverse** parties would contaminate the anchoring claim, because the entire rationale for diversity jurisdiction — protecting against state-court bias toward the in-state party — disappears once there are parties from the same state on both sides of the case.

Therefore, AIC-deficient claims are permissible under § 1367(a) so long as the anchoring claim retains original jurisdiction.

**Why the single-defendant limit matters under § 1367(b):** Section 1367(b) ousts supplemental jurisdiction over claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rule 20. If there are multiple defendants, Rule 20 was necessary to join them, so plaintiffs' claims against those additional defendants fall within § 1367(b)'s ouster. A single defendant, however, was not joined via Rule 20; only the multiple plaintiffs were joined (via Rule 20 in simple joinder or Rule 23 in a class action). Thus the § 1367(b) ouster does not apply.

Complete diversity must be preserved at all times under *Strawbridge v. Curtiss*. <!-- corrected: "Strawberry Justice Curtis" → Strawbridge v. Curtiss -->

### Illustrative Hypothetical

In the class hypothetical, there was complete diversity between the named plaintiff and the single defendant, and the named plaintiff was AIC-sufficient. Therefore it did not matter that unnamed class members were both non-diverse and AIC-deficient: under *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur*, diversity is assessed only as to named plaintiffs; and under *Exxon Mobil v. Allapattah*, the supplemental jurisdiction statute permits jurisdiction over even non-diverse unnamed class members whose claims are AIC-deficient.

## Personal Jurisdiction in Class Actions

**Unnamed plaintiff class members:** As long as sufficient procedural protections are in place — as required for class certification under Rule 23 — it is permissible for a court to exercise personal jurisdiction over unnamed plaintiff class members. That is the rule from *Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts*. <!-- corrected: "shuts" → Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts -->

**Open question — specific personal jurisdiction:** An open question remains when only some class members' claims have minimum contacts relating the subject matter, the forum, and the defendant. We know from *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court* <!-- corrected: "Bristol-Myers Quib" → Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court --> that, in a **simple joinder** context, a state court cannot exercise specific personal jurisdiction over claims by plaintiffs who lack the necessary minimum contacts merely because other plaintiffs in the same case do have those contacts. Because Rule 4(k)(1)(A) limits a federal court's personal jurisdiction to what a state court of the same state could exercise, this rule applies in federal court in simple joinder cases as well.

Whether *Bristol-Myers Squibb* applies to **class actions** is unresolved. The answer turns on whether a certified class is:

- An **aggregation of individual claims** (class action as joinder on steroids), in which case *Bristol-Myers Squibb* would prohibit specific personal jurisdiction as to individual class members whose claims lack minimum contacts; or
- A **new legal entity**, in which case that single entity has been harmed in many locations and could likely assert specific personal jurisdiction wherever any of its members (at least the named plaintiffs) suffered injury.

The Supreme Court has not yet decided which characterization is correct.

**General personal jurisdiction** remains straightforward: if you are suing a single defendant at its principal place of business or its state of incorporation, general personal jurisdiction is available for all class members' claims.

## Venue in Class Actions

For a class action against a single defendant that is a corporation, 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c) and (b)(1) together provide that venue can be laid wherever the defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction (since a corporation resides, for venue purposes, wherever it is subject to personal jurisdiction in that district). It may also be possible to lay venue under § 1391(b)(2) in a district where a key event common to all class members occurred — for example, the district where the alleged computer programming that swept money from plaintiffs' accounts was carried out.

## Pleading

The class action complaint must of course comply with *Twombly* and *Iqbal*. <!-- corrected: "Trombone and Eccle" → Twombly and Iqbal -->

## Rule 23 Class Certification

### The Four Prerequisites: Rule 23(a)

To certify a class, all four Rule 23(a) prerequisites must be satisfied, and the class must fit within at least one Rule 23(b) category.

#### Numerosity — Rule 23(a)(1)

- Generally satisfied if there are more than 40 plaintiffs.
- Generally not satisfied if there are fewer than 20 plaintiffs.
- Between 20 and 40, courts are more likely to certify if plaintiffs are geographically dispersed (making simple joinder impractical) or if their individual amounts in controversy are small (making individual suits not worth filing).

#### Commonality — Rule 23(a)(2)

Commonality is the most demanding prerequisite, especially after *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*. <!-- corrected: "Walmart" → Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes --> *Wal-Mart* requires **common answers** to common questions — ordinarily some specific practice or policy that the defendant has engaged in with respect to each and every class member. The mere existence of subjectivity in individual employment decisions (as in *Wal-Mart* itself, where there was no common company-wide policy for hiring and promotion decisions) is not itself a policy. You must be able to point to something concrete that the defendant did in common to all class members.

**Horizontal choice of law and commonality:** Choice-of-law issues can defeat commonality. Each class member's claim is treated as a separate claim for choice-of-law purposes. Under *Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Manufacturing Co.* <!-- corrected: "Claxon" → *Klaxon* --> and *Ferens v. John Deere Co.*, <!-- corrected: "John Deere" → *Ferens v. John Deere Co.* --> a federal court applies the choice-of-law rules of the state in which it sits. That means different states' substantive law may apply to different class members' claims, and those differences can affect elements of the cause of action or available defenses — potentially defeating commonality.

State courts have sometimes tried to solve this problem by applying a single state's law to all class members. The Supreme Court held in *Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts* that a state court cannot apply its own law to class members' claims unless there is some contact relating the defendant, the subject matter of the litigation, and the forum. The constitutional floor for choice-of-law contacts is lower than for personal jurisdiction, but some contact is required. In *Shutts*, Kansas had no contact whatsoever with the oil leases in Texas and Oklahoma, so Kansas could not apply Kansas law.

In the federal court context, even if a state's choice-of-law rules would seem to authorize applying a single state's law, a federal court may be constitutionally prevented from doing so unless there are contacts relating all unnamed class members to that state.

The result may be that many different states' laws apply to different class members' claims, which could defeat commonality — though not necessarily, since the trial judge always retains discretion about how much commonality is enough. The bar from *Wal-Mart* is high but not impossible. *Shady Grove Orthopedic Associates v. Allstate Insurance Co.* is a solid case for finding commonality. <!-- corrected: "Shady grow" → Shady Grove Orthopedic Associates v. Allstate Insurance Co. -->

#### Typicality and Adequacy of Representation — Rule 23(a)(3) and (4)

Typicality and representativeness tend to merge in practice. The inquiry is whether the named plaintiff's theory of relief is sufficiently close to the theories of relief of the unnamed class members such that if the named plaintiff pushes hard on its claim, all unnamed class members will necessarily benefit.

For **adequacy of representation**, the key question is whether there are conflicts of interest among class members. If some class members want immediate money (for treatment) while others want money preserved for later (in case they become sick), there may be a conflict requiring separate subclasses and separate representatives.

This issue arose in *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, <!-- corrected: "ANCAM" → Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor --> where a large class of asbestos-exposure plaintiffs included both people who were currently sick and those who were showing signs but were not yet sick. The Court held that the class failed on adequacy of representation because of that conflict.

### Rule 23(b) Categories

The class must also satisfy at least one Rule 23(b) category:

**Rule 23(b)(1)(A) and Rule 23(b)(2)** are essentially the same: they apply when the defendant's course of conduct is common to all class members such that injunctive relief is the appropriate remedy and that is all that is sought. Neither of these categories applied in the class hypothetical.

**Rule 23(b)(1)(B) — Limited fund:** Applies when a limited fund (such as an insurance policy at its limits) cannot pay all class members. Rather than allowing individual plaintiffs to race to judgment and exhaust the fund, a mandatory class (no opt-out) is certified so that all class members receive a proportional share. This is analogous to a bankruptcy proceeding. Also not applicable in the hypothetical.

**Rule 23(b)(3) — Damages class:** Requires two additional elements:
1. **Predominance:** The common issues of law or fact must predominate over the non-common issues. This is a higher bar than commonality — if a class satisfies predominance, it necessarily satisfies commonality.
2. **Superiority:** The class action device must be superior to other methods of adjudication. The baseline assumption is that individual plaintiffs should ordinarily retain control over their own claims, so superiority must be affirmatively shown — for example, that individual lawsuits are economically impractical given small per-plaintiff recoveries.

Note: There is much more to class actions — notice, opt-out rights, settlement approval — that was not covered and will not be tested on the exam. Further study is available in advanced civil procedure coursework.

## Beacon Theaters and the Right to a Jury Trial

The rule from *Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover* is straightforward.

**Background principle:** Under general preclusion principles and *Parklane Hosiery*, if lawsuit one is an equity lawsuit tried to the court (with the judge as fact-finder), the judge's factual findings can serve as the basis for issue preclusion in lawsuit two — even if lawsuit two has a right to a jury trial and issue preclusion would thereby deprive a party of having a jury determine those factual issues. The Supreme Court in *Parklane Hosiery* says that is permissible.

**The Beacon Theaters rule:** However, given the flexible modern rules of civil procedure — broad joinder, counterclaims, and so forth — if an equity claim and a law claim (the latter carrying a right to a jury trial) are in the **same** lawsuit and present common issues, a judge may **not** use scheduling power to try the equity claim first, make factual findings without a jury, and thereby deprive a party of its right to have a jury determine those same common factual issues. That is an abuse of discretion.

In *Beacon Theatres* itself, the court scheduled the old theater's equity claim (seeking an anti-lawsuit injunction) before the new theater's damages claim (which was on the law side and subject to a jury trial right), and would thereby have prevented the new theater from getting a jury trial on common factual issues. The Supreme Court held that scheduling power must be used to **preserve** the right to jury trial, not to circumvent it.

The general implication is that federal courts should schedule trials in a way that makes juries available on law claims whenever possible. There is additional case law beyond this course that adds nuance, but that is the framework for this class.

## Erie Doctrine: Review via Shady Grove

### The Four-Part Framework

When you encounter an Erie issue on an exam, run through all four Erie tests:

1. The York test
2. The Hanna majority test — Scalia version
3. The Hanna majority test — Ginsburg version
4. Justice Harlan's concurrence in *Hanna v. Plumer*

In terms of the actual black-letter law that courts apply after this course, the Court appears to have settled 9-0 on what we call the **Hanna majority framework**, though it is not settled which version governs.

### The Hanna Majority Framework

**Step 1 — Identify the source of federal law.** If the source is constitutional law, statutory law, or a Rule of Civil Procedure, apply the **valid and applicable** test: if the federal law is valid and applies to the situation, it governs under the Supremacy Clause regardless of contrary state law.

- **Constitutional provisions:** Validity is presumed; they are generally not interpreted narrowly for applicability purposes.
- **Federal statutes:** Validity is governed by whether there is a constitutional provision empowering Congress to enact the statute. For statutes affecting federal court procedure, the power to create the lower federal courts combined with the Necessary and Proper Clause empowers Congress to make housekeeping or procedural rules applicable in federal courts. Congress may also use its substantive powers (e.g., Commerce Clause) to provide rules of decision for substantive matters. As to applicability, courts are generally not eager to construe a federal statute narrowly to get out of the way of a state rule of decision — though Justice Ginsburg would do so more readily than others.
- **Rules of Civil Procedure:** Validity is governed by the Rules Enabling Act, specifically 28 U.S.C. § 2072, which provides that a rule "shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right." The Court appears thoroughly settled on the *Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.* <!-- corrected: "SIVAC" → Sibbach v. Wilson & Co. --> test for interpreting that language — an atextual interpretation that asks whether the rule "really regulates procedure." Because all rules do regulate procedure, as long as you cite § 2072 and the *Sibbach* test and conclude the rule is valid, you will be fine.

**Step 2 — Applicability (where Scalia and Ginsburg diverge):**

- **Scalia's version:** Consult only the federal rule itself. If the rule applies according to its plain language, apply it.
- **Ginsburg's version (supported by four other Justices, a total of five in *Shady Grove*):** Interpret the rule with sensitivity to state interests. This may mean construing the rule narrowly or in a way that allows the state policy to be effectuated.

In *Shady Grove*, Justice Ginsburg was unable to persuade Justice Stevens to join her in narrowly construing Rule 23. Justice Stevens agreed with Ginsburg's interpretive approach but disagreed with its application, and thereby provided the fifth vote for the result that Rule 23 governs in a situation where a New York statute purported to function as a class-action bar in certain circumstances.

**Step 3 — Federal common law (if no constitutional, statutory, or Rules source applies):** Use the twin aims of Erie: (1) discouraging forum shopping and (2) avoiding inequitable administration of the laws. The aim that does the real work is forum shopping — what causes plaintiffs' lawyers to forum shop is the opportunity to get a different and better result in one court versus another. That is the York test: whether there is an outcome-determinative difference between the federal and state approaches.

### Broader Policy Questions

The bigger-picture questions in the access-to-justice module are more policy-oriented:

- If the federal (and much of the state) court system is effectively unusable by ordinary individuals without substantial resources — accessible primarily to wealthy individuals and to institutions that can marshal the necessary resources — what is its legitimacy?
- If legitimacy rests only on the threat of government enforcement rather than on genuine accessibility and the rule of law, is that satisfactory, and if not, what should be done?

Some have advocated for overturning or limiting *Turner* and *Lassiter* and having the court system recognize a civil right to counsel. Severe challenges to that approach are discussed in the readings, including the fact that the constitutional right to counsel in criminal cases — where the stakes are arguably at least as high, especially in capital cases — has not produced reliably high-quality representation or adequate funding. There also appears to be limited political will to extend such a right to civil cases.

What other measures short of a civil right to counsel might address these access-to-justice problems? Even for those who believe a civil right to counsel is an appropriate aspiration, it is worth thinking about what should be done in the meantime.

## Introduction

This recording provides a broad overview of the topics covered in Civil Procedure across the semester, organized by class day. ## Days 1–4: Subject Matter Jurisdiction of the Federal Courts

[00:00:00] State courts are, as a baseline, always willing to hear any type of dispute. The question we address in days one through four is: even though a case can almost always begin in state court, when is it *also* permissible for it to begin in federal court?

The doctrine that answers this question is **subject matter jurisdiction** — specifically, the subject matter jurisdiction of the lower federal courts (the federal district courts).

### Day 1 — Arising Under Jurisdiction (§ 1331)

The first basis for federal subject matter jurisdiction is **arising under jurisdiction**, governed by 28 U.S.C. § 1331. A federal court has jurisdiction when the plaintiff's cause of action was created by federal law. Our principal case is *Motley*, which establishes this narrow construction and introduces the **well-pleaded complaint rule**. We then examine the *Smith-Grable* exception, under which a state law cause of action may still support arising-under jurisdiction when a federal issue is necessarily embedded within it. *Smith* and *Grable* illustrate this exception.

Congress also designates certain case types as **exclusive federal jurisdiction**, meaning they must be filed in federal court — primarily federal antitrust, intellectual property (patents and copyrights), and suits against the United States.

### Day 2 — Diversity Jurisdiction (§ 1332)

The second basis is **diversity jurisdiction**, which turns not on the subject matter of the dispute but on the citizenship of the parties. The parties must be citizens of different states. For purposes of § 1332, citizenship means **domicile**, not formal state citizenship. Rules differ for corporations (principal place of business and state of incorporation) and unincorporated associations.

### Day 3 — Supplemental Jurisdiction (§ 1367)

When a plaintiff wants to assert multiple claims in federal court but only some have an independent basis of subject matter jurisdiction (the "anchoring claims"), **supplemental jurisdiction** under § 1367 allows the court to hear the remaining "would-be supplemental" claims — provided they form part of the same constitutional case or controversy. Section 1367(b) imposes limits on plaintiff-side claims in diversity cases.

### Day 4 — Removal and Abstention

**Removal** is the doctrine by which a defendant moves a case filed in state court to federal district court. Only defendants may remove; there is no mechanism to move a case from federal back to state court. We also address **abstention** — circumstances in which a federal court, despite having subject matter jurisdiction, declines to exercise it.

---

## Day 5: Horizontal Choice of Law

[00:20:00] When a legal transaction or tort crosses a state boundary, which state's law governs the dispute? This is **horizontal choice of law** — horizontal because it involves a choice between states, as opposed to vertical choice of law (between state and federal law).

*Example*: A Massachusetts resident drives into New Hampshire and injures someone's dog. When the resulting lawsuit is filed, does New Hampshire law or Massachusetts law govern the negligence claim?

---

## Days 6–8: Personal Jurisdiction

Days six through eight address geography — specifically, in which court, located where, can a lawsuit properly be brought?

**Personal jurisdiction** answers the question: can *this* court, in *this* place, exercise power over this defendant? A court in Alaska has no personal jurisdiction over a Massachusetts resident for conduct that occurred entirely in New Hampshire and has no connection to Alaska.

We will trace personal jurisdiction doctrine from its territorial origins through the modern minimum-contacts framework established in *International Shoe*.

---

## Day 9: Venue and Forum Non-Convenience

[00:35:00] **Venue** is another geographic doctrine, operating entirely within the federal court system. It specifies which federal district a case must be filed in. More importantly, we explore how cases can be moved:

- **Transfers** move cases between federal courts within the United States.
- **Forum non-convenience** is used when the suggestion is to dismiss a state or federal court case so the plaintiff may refile in a court in another state or abroad. There is no mechanism to transfer directly from one state court to another.

---

## Days 10–11: The Erie Doctrine — Vertical Choice of Law

After a case called *Erie Railroad v. Tompkins*, the Erie doctrine governs the **vertical choice of law** question: when a case is in federal court, which law does the court apply — state law or federal law?

This question arises constantly in diversity cases. The framework for answering it — the York test, the Hanna majority test, and Justice Harlan's concurrence — will occupy much of days ten and eleven.

---

## Days 11–12: The Complaint, Pleading Standards, and Preliminary Injunctions

[00:50:00] Once we know where a lawsuit will proceed and what law applies, we walk the lawsuit through its lifecycle.

The first document is the **complaint**. How specific must it be? How much factual detail must a plaintiff provide before having the opportunity to gather evidence from the defendant through discovery? We will work through notice pleading, code pleading, and the modern Supreme Court's plausibility pleading standard from *Twombly* and *Iqbal*.

Day 12 introduces **preliminary injunctions** — orders a plaintiff seeks before winning the case, asking the court to prevent irreparable harm while the litigation is pending. Courts are reluctant to grant relief before the merits have been established, so they require the plaintiff to satisfy a demanding four-factor test.

---

## Days 13–14: Service of Process, Procedural Due Process, and Discovery

**Day 13** addresses two related questions: What notice must a defendant receive of the lawsuit? And what opportunity to be heard must the court provide before taking action against the defendant? These questions arise both in civil litigation and in the administrative state — for example, under what circumstances may the Social Security Administration terminate disability benefits, and what process must it offer? This falls under the doctrine of **procedural due process**.

**Day 14** introduces **discovery** — the set of tools (interrogatories, depositions, requests for production and admission) that allow parties to compel each other and third parties to produce information after a complaint has been filed.

---

## Day 15: Summary Judgment

After discovery, the question is: do we need a trial? If the facts are sufficiently clear and there is no genuine issue of material fact, the court can resolve the litigation without trial through a **motion for summary judgment**.

---

## Days 16–17: The Jury, the Judge, and Allocation of Decision-Making Power

[01:05:00] **Day 16** takes up which cases entitle a party to a jury trial. There is a historical division between actions at law (right to jury) and actions in equity (no right to jury). Even when a jury is available, issues of fact are for the jury while issues of law are for the judge.

**Day 17** examines the tools by which a judge can intervene even after a jury has spoken — including motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) and motions for a new trial.

---

## Day 18: Appellate Jurisdiction

When does a right to appeal arise? The federal system follows a **final judgment rule** under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 — the case must proceed essentially to final judgment before appeal is available. This contrasts with many state systems (such as New York's) that permit broad interlocutory appeals. We examine the exceptions in the federal system: § 1292(a), § 1292(b), the collateral order doctrine, and mandamus.

---

## Days 19–22: Multiple Parties and Preclusion

**Day 19** asks: when is it permissible to have multiple plaintiffs and defendants in a single lawsuit? We also begin **preclusion**.

**Preclusion** doctrine governs when a concluded lawsuit bars re-litigation in a subsequent suit. There are two types:

- **Claim preclusion** (res judicata): prevents re-litigation of the entire claim that was or could have been litigated.
- **Issue preclusion** (collateral estoppel): prevents re-litigation of a specific factual issue that was actually litigated and decided.

*Example*: I drive into New Hampshire and hit both a person and their dog. The dog owner sues for damage to the dog, loses, and then files a second suit for personal injuries. Can that second suit proceed? Can the value of the dog be re-litigated in a later case?

We also address what happens when the preclusion law of the rendering court differs from that of the subsequent court — inter-jurisdictional preclusion problems.

[01:20:00] **Class actions** are aggregation devices: a single plaintiff represents thousands or millions of similarly situated individuals in a single lawsuit. We cover the requirements of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23 and the policy debates surrounding aggregate litigation.

# SOURCE: content/course/lectures-videos/holder-in-due-course.md — Holder in Due Course Doctrine

## Overview and Course Context

This video explains a doctrine from commercial transactions law called the Holder in Due Course Doctrine. We will encounter it again later in the semester — in particular for class period nine, in the form of *Swift v. Tyson*, which is a precursor case to *Erie*.

## Historical Background: The Problem with Hard Currency

To understand the Holder in Due Course Doctrine, consider the world 150 to 200 years ago. Banks were not common, and traveling to one — especially if you lived outside a major city — was a significant ordeal. Hard currency (dollar bills and coins) was equally difficult to come by. Much of the economy still relied on barter and trade, which are inefficient and make transacting business difficult.

To develop a functioning economy, you need a mechanism that allows people to transact without barter. That mechanism often requires credit: the idea that you can have something now and pay for it later. Around this time, the concept of memorializing credit in writing began to develop — promises to pay, and eventually, as banks emerged, what we now call checks.

## How Checks Work

A check is a piece of paper you write out to someone. Notably, it says "pay to the order of" the named person — not simply "pay this person." That phrasing is intentional. It gives the person receiving the check the right to order you (the check writer) to pay someone. And critically, that right can be passed along.

Instead of having to go to a bank to convert the check into cash, the person receiving it can endorse it over to someone else — essentially ordering the check writer to pay a new person. That new person can do the same, and so on. The check itself functions as currency, circulating from hand to hand without ever being deposited.

This was highly practical in an era when getting to a bank was difficult. Under commercial law, a check doesn't even need to be written on paper — technically, writing one on a shirt would still be valid. The point is that checks were designed to move as freely as money.

## The Swindle Problem

The difficulty arises as a check moves further from the original transaction. Suppose the person who originally received the check obtained it through fraud — by swindling the check writer. If the check goes no further and the check writer discovers the fraud, the writer can go to their bank and issue a stop payment order, blocking the fraudulent transaction.

But once the swindler endorses the check over to a third party in exchange for something of value, the problem compounds. That third party had no knowledge of the original fraud. If people downstream worry that any given check might be subject to a stop payment order, they will either refuse to accept checks or discount their value. A $10 check might only be worth $7.50 to someone who has to factor in the risk that it won't be honored. This undermines the entire purpose of allowing checks to circulate as currency.

## The Holder in Due Course Doctrine

To solve this problem, the law — developed primarily in the United Kingdom — created the **Holder in Due Course Doctrine**. The doctrine provides that if you are a downstream recipient of a check (the third, fourth, or tenth person to receive it, not the original alleged swindler), you can require the check writer to honor the check. You can override the stop payment order and demand payment.

The reasoning is straightforward: you had no knowledge of the original swindle, and you gave value in exchange for the check. The check writer, who did have direct dealings with the swindler, is in a far better position to identify and pursue the swindler through litigation. So the law says: you (the check writer) must pay the downstream holder, and then you go find and sue the swindler yourself.

This doctrine makes downstream parties more willing to accept checks at full face value, which is essential to making checks function as a reliable medium of exchange.

## Illustrative Example: Batgirl, the Joker, Thor, and Black Widow

Consider the following transaction chain:

- **Batgirl** (domiciled in South Carolina) writes a check "pay to the order of" the **Joker** in exchange for land — which, as it turns out, does not exist. The Joker has swindled her.
- Before Batgirl discovers the fraud, the **Joker** endorses the check over to **Thor** in exchange for a hammer.
- **Thor** endorses the check over to **Black Widow** in exchange for the Tesseract.

At the point Black Widow receives the check, Batgirl discovers the fraud. She contacts her bank and issues a stop payment order. When Black Widow tries to deposit the check, payment is refused.

Black Widow (domiciled in Massachusetts) then sues Batgirl in federal court — a diversity case. The question becomes: what law applies?

## Connection to *Swift v. Tyson* and the Erie Question

Some states at the time *Swift v. Tyson* was decided did not recognize the Holder in Due Course Doctrine. Those states held that the original fraud — the failure of consideration in the transaction between the swindler and the check writer — relieved the check writer of any obligation to honor the check, regardless of who held it downstream.

In *Swift v. Tyson*, the underlying facts follow this same pattern:

- **Swindlers** obtain a loan from a **creditor** (Swift).
- As part of the transaction, the swindlers convey nonexistent land to a **guarantor**, who in exchange guarantees repayment of the loan.
- The swindlers then pay the creditor via a check drawn on the guarantor.
- The swindlers disappear. The guarantor discovers the fraud, stops payment on the check, and refuses to honor the guarantee.
- The creditor sues the guarantor.

The legal question: Does New York state law — which did not recognize the Holder in Due Course Doctrine — apply? Or does some body of federal common law apply, which might recognize the doctrine?

More fundamentally: is there such a thing as federal common law that a federal court sitting in diversity can apply? That is the question at the heart of *Swift v. Tyson*, and ultimately the question that *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins* will answer.

## Overview

This is a short supplemental video explaining the passing-on defense — a doctrine that defendants asserted in Sherman Antitrust Act § 1 cases during the early and mid-twentieth century. Understanding the passing-on defense is necessary background for one of the appellate jurisdiction rulings covered in the next class period.

The Supreme Court ultimately ruled that the passing-on defense is not available in response to a Sherman Act § 1 lawsuit. But for a period before that ruling, the question was unsettled, and two appellate jurisdiction cases in the casebook arose out of that uncertainty.

---

## How Horizontal Price-Fixing Works

Section 1 of the Sherman Antitrust Act prohibits agreements among competitors that restrain trade. A classic example is horizontal price-fixing: competitors at the same level of the supply chain agree, rather than competing, to charge a single fixed price.

The scenario used in class: several suppliers of natural gas agree among themselves on the price they will charge a utility. Instead of competing and driving the price down, they form an illegal horizontal agreement — "horizontal" because they are all on the same plane in the supply chain, selling the same product to the same buyer. As a result, the utility must pay artificially inflated prices for gas. The utility then burns the gas, generates electricity, and sells the electricity to customers.

In a diagram:

- **Suppliers 1–4** (connected by arrows representing the illegal agreement) → sell gas to
- **The utility** (at artificially high prices) → which sells electricity to
- **Customers A, B, C, D...**

---

## The Passing-On Defense

When the utility sues the suppliers for violating Sherman Act § 1, the suppliers might assert that the utility was not actually harmed — arguing as follows:

The utility is itself a regulated monopoly. State governments recognize that because of the enormous fixed costs of building and operating utility infrastructure, it makes sense to have only one utility serving a given area. Customers cannot go elsewhere. So when the suppliers charged the utility inflated prices for gas, the utility simply passed those higher costs along to its customers in the form of higher electricity rates. The utility's profits were unaffected. The customers bore the real harm.

Under this argument, the utility has no damages and no standing to sue — or at least no recoverable injury. The customers are the ones harmed, but they are not parties to the utility's lawsuit. The suppliers would wait for the customers to sue, but in the meantime would defend the utility's claim on the ground that the utility suffered no injury because it passed the overcharge on.

That argument — that a direct purchaser was not injured because it passed its higher costs downstream to its own customers — is called the **passing-on defense**.

Note: In antitrust litigation, damages are sometimes trebled ("treble damages"), multiplying the award by three. This magnifies the stakes when the passing-on defense is at issue.

---

## Why the Passing-On Defense Created Appellate Jurisdiction Questions

Once a utility sued suppliers and the suppliers wished to assert the passing-on defense, they would seek discovery from the utility on the question of whether it actually passed the higher gas prices on to customers. The suppliers would want documents such as the utility's pricing policies, its customer margins, how it sets rates, and its internal economic records.

The utility would resist this discovery, arguing that the passing-on defense is legally unavailable, making any discovery on the question outside the permissible scope. The suppliers would respond that the defense is available and the discovery is relevant.

This discovery dispute forces the trial judge to rule on a threshold legal question: Is the passing-on defense legally available? The ruling has asymmetric consequences:

- If the judge rules the defense **is available**, the suppliers may take broad, burdensome, expensive discovery from the utility.
- If the judge rules the defense **is not available**, there is no discovery burden at all — at least with respect to this defense.

This legal question, arising in the context of a discovery ruling, is the kind of ruling that — before the Supreme Court settled the merits question — could give rise to appellate jurisdiction issues. Those cases are the subject of the next class period.

## Overview

This video explains the relationship among service of process, the filing of lawsuits, and the concept of commencement — all against the backdrop of statutes of limitations. This material is background for *Hanna v. Plumer* and *Walker v. Armco Steel Corp.*

---

## What a Limitations Period Requires: Commencement

[00:00:00] A statute of limitations requires that a plaintiff **commence** a lawsuit within a specified time after a triggering event (an injury, a breach, etc.). The statute of limitations will typically say something like: "A plaintiff must commence a lawsuit within two years of [the triggering event]."

The critical question is: what counts as commencement?

Two things must happen to get a lawsuit going:
1. **File** papers with the court — notifying the court that a lawsuit exists.
2. **Serve** the defendant — notifying the defendant that it has been sued.

Generally, filing precedes service, though some state systems require service before filing. We will work through the more common sequence: file, then serve.

---

## Methods of Service

[00:10:00] Every court specifies permissible methods of service in its procedural rules.

**In-hand service (the "tag")**: Physically handing papers to the defendant in person. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4(e)(2)(A), this is permissible but not the only method. Some states, for certain actions, require the tag as the *only* permissible method.

**Substituted service at residence**: Leaving a copy of the complaint at the defendant's home with a responsible adult present. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4(e)(2)(B), this is permissible in federal court. In *Hanna v. Plumer*, the relevant state statute permitted only in-hand service for the type of action at issue — leaving papers with a spouse at the defendant's home was insufficient under state law.

---

## What Happens When Service is Defective

If a plaintiff fails to comply with the court's service rules, a defendant who nonetheless learns of the lawsuit may move to dismiss for improper service. If that motion succeeds, the lawsuit is dismissed.

The dismissal is not preclusive — the plaintiff may refile. The real danger is that by the time the plaintiff gets around to refiling and serving properly, the **limitations period may have passed**. If so, the defendant will move to dismiss the refiled lawsuit as untimely, and that motion will succeed. A dismissal for failure to properly serve process effectively kills the entire lawsuit if it comes too late for a proper refile within the limitations period.

---

## The Grace Period: Filing Before the Deadline, Serving After

[00:25:00] Both federal and state court rules generally allow a plaintiff who **files before the limitations deadline** a period of time after filing within which to serve process. If service is completed within that window, the lawsuit is deemed **commenced as of the day of filing** — not the day of service.

**How it works**: Suppose the limitations period is two years and you file on the last day (day 730 of the 730-day period). If you then serve the defendant within the grace period, you are deemed to have commenced the lawsuit on day 730 — within the limitations period — and the case proceeds.

If you fail to serve within the grace period, the defendant will move to dismiss for failure to comply with service rules. That motion will be granted. If you then try to refile, the new filing is outside the limitations period, and the defendant will successfully move to dismiss the second lawsuit as untimely.

---

## The *Hanna* and *Walker* Problem: Federal vs. State Grace Periods

[00:35:00] This is where state and federal rules may diverge in two ways:

### Difference 1: Length of the Grace Period

At the time of *Walker v. Armco Steel Corp.*, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4(m) provided a **120-day** grace period after filing. The relevant state rule provided a **60-day** grace period.

If a plaintiff files on the last day of the limitations period and serves on day 75, the plaintiff has complied with the federal rule (served within 120 days) but not the state rule (served outside the 60-day window). Whether the lawsuit was timely "commenced" depends on which grace period governs — a classic Erie problem.

### Difference 2: Permissible Methods of Service

Under the federal rules, leaving papers at the defendant's home with a responsible adult is permissible. Under the Massachusetts statute at issue in *Hanna v. Plumer*, only in-hand service was permissible for that type of action.

If the plaintiff leaves papers with the defendant's spouse and the state rule requires in-hand service, was service proper? Again, the answer depends on which rule governs — a classic Erie problem.

These two scenarios set up the Erie analysis we will work through in *Hanna v. Plumer* and *Walker v. Armco Steel Corp.* in class 10.

## Overview

## The Problem Trusts Solve

[00:00:00] Rich people who do not want to spend their time managing their own money can create a separate legal entity — a **trust** — transfer money into it, and then hire someone to manage the money on the trust's behalf.

The trust is a separate legal entity, somewhat like a corporation. It holds assets and requires a human agent to direct its decisions. That agent is the **trustee**, engaged by contract — analogous to the way a corporation engages directors and officers.

---

## Structure of a Trust

[00:05:00] The basic arrangement:

1. **Settlor** (the rich person who creates the trust) transfers money or assets into the trust.
2. The trust hires a **trustee** to manage the assets — to invest, to make decisions about the portfolio.
3. The trust generates income through those investments.
4. The trustee distributes income to **beneficiaries** according to the settlor's instructions.

**Two types of beneficiaries:**
- **Life beneficiary**: Receives income during some period, often the settlor's lifetime.
- **Death beneficiary**: Receives the remaining trust assets (or ongoing income) upon the settlor's death.

Settlors often name themselves as the life beneficiary — essentially directing the trustee to pay them the investment returns while alive — and name children, grandchildren, or other heirs as the death beneficiaries. This is exactly what the wealthy woman in *Hanson v. Denckla* did: she named herself as life beneficiary and designated some (but not all) of her descendants as death beneficiaries.

---

## Why Use a Trust? (Tax Reasons)

[00:15:00] The primary reason is tax treatment. Assets transferred to a trust before death are no longer the settlor's property — they belong to a separate legal entity. They therefore fall outside the **estate** at death and receive more favorable tax treatment than assets that pass through probate. The estate consists only of assets still legally owned by the decedent at death.

A key condition: for the trust to receive this favorable treatment, the settlor must genuinely give up control. The settlor can direct *who* receives income (by designating beneficiaries), but **cannot direct how the trustee invests** — cannot instruct the trustee to buy specific bonds, stocks, or real estate, and certainly cannot instruct the trustee to purchase personal property for the settlor's own use. If the settlor retains effective control over the trust corpus, a court may find the trust invalidly created, collapsing the supposed separation between the person and the money.

---

## Wills and Probate: Contrast

[00:22:00] A will directs what happens to a decedent's **estate** — the assets legally owned at death. Persons who benefit under a will are called **legatees**. The estate goes through **probate**: a court-supervised process for identifying assets, paying debts, and distributing the remainder per the will's instructions.

Assets that passed into a trust before death are **not part of the estate** and do not go through probate. This is often the tax and estate-planning point of using a trust.

---

## Litigation Risks: Undue Influence and Trustee Misconduct

[00:28:00] Because large sums of money are involved and the arrangements are complex, trust structures generate a great deal of litigation:

**Undue influence**: When a settlor — especially an elderly person — creates a trust or designates beneficiaries, there is a risk that someone exercised undue influence over the settlor's decisions. In *Hanson v. Denckla*, the question is whether someone exercised undue influence either in the creation of the trust or in the designation of death beneficiaries.

**Trustee misconduct**: A trustee sits on someone else's money. The risk is self-dealing — for example, directing the trust to invest in the trustee's own business, putting the trustee in a conflict of interest. The trustee's duty is to act in the best interests of the beneficiaries; making favorable loans to one's own business at the trust's expense breaches that duty.

## Relevance to Personal Jurisdiction

The specific legal issue that makes trust arrangements important in a civil procedure course is **personal jurisdiction over the trustee**. In *Hanson v. Denckla*, the central question is whether Florida courts can exercise personal jurisdiction over a Delaware trustee to resolve a dispute about the trust's beneficiaries. The trust was created in Delaware; the settlor later moved to Florida. The trustee never acted in Florida and never had any contact with Florida — the trustee only had contacts with Delaware, where it was incorporated and conducted all of its trust administration.

## Lead-Up to *Erie*

### *Swift v. Tyson*

**Background:** Swindle (started by Norton and Keith) eventually leads to Swift suing Tyson in NY federal court based on diversity. Rules of Decision Act (28 U.S.C. § 1652) directs federal courts to apply the "laws of the several states." Under NY state common law, the original swindle relieved Tyson of the obligation to pay. But Justice Story thinks the better rule is the "holder in due course" doctrine, under which Tyson had to pay Swift and sue the swindlers himself.

**Issue:** Does the federal court have to apply NY state common law (under which Tyson would not have to pay), because "laws of the several states" in § 1652 encompasses state common law?

**Holding:** No. State common law does not constitute the "law of the several states." Federal courts aren't bound to follow state common law, and can consider general common-law principles and create a federal body of common law decisions in cases where there is a conflict between state and federal law.

<!-- page: 4 -->

### *Black & White Taxicab & Transfer Co. v. Brown & Yellow Taxicab & Transfer Co.*

**Background:** Brown & Yellow Taxicab (KY) forms an agreement with a railroad giving them exclusive privilege of soliciting passengers at the station. This agreement is invalid under KY state common law. Competitor Black & White Taxicab (KY) begins soliciting passengers at the station. Brown & Yellow reincorporated in TN <!-- uncertain: original may read "TE" — likely "TN" (Tennessee) --> to invoke diversity jurisdiction and sue the competitor, hoping to take advantage of federal common law under which the agreement was valid.

**Issue:** Can Brown & Yellow obtain an injunction under federal common law after reincorporating?

**Holding:** Yes. The federal court in KY granted Brown & Yellow an injunction under federal common law, and the judgment was affirmed on appeal.

**Why this is bad:** Don't want people to be able to manufacture diversity to seek better legal rules when existing law is unfavorable to them.

<!-- page: 5 -->

## *Erie RR v. Tompkins*

**Facts:** Tompkins was injured while walking on a footpath parallel to Erie Railroad tracks in PA. Lawsuit was filed against Erie R.R. in federal court. Under PA state common law, Tompkins would be precluded from recovering because he would be treated as a trespasser (to whom no duty of care would be owed). The federal district court concluded that the railroad did have an ordinary duty of care, so Tompkins could recover.

**Issue:** Does the federal district court have to apply PA state common law (under which Tompkins cannot recover), or can the court create its own federal general common law?

**Holding:** There is no federal general common law. *Swift* is overturned: federal courts must apply state law for substantive matters (when sitting in diversity or when a state law is otherwise before federal courts) and federal law for procedural matters. The "laws of the several states" under § 1652 encompasses state common law.

## Twin Aims of *Erie*

1. Forum shopping
2. Inequitable administration of law

## Policy Justifications for *Erie*

1. **What is law?**
   - There is no pre-existing "natural" law to be discovered.

2. **Statutory (28 U.S.C. § 1652)**
   - State common law necessarily qualifies as "laws of the several states" of § 1652.

3. **Constitutional**
   - **Federalism**
     - States should be primary regulators of behavior.
     - We care more about vertical uniformity (state court ↔ federal court in same jurisdiction) than horizontal uniformity (federal court in one jurisdiction ↔ federal court in another jurisdiction).
   - **Separation of Powers**
     - Federal courts shouldn't make up rules — that is the legislature's role.
   - **Equal Protection**
     - Difference in substantive law for in-state and out-of-state plaintiffs violates equal protection.

4. **Federal court prudence**
   - In the *Swift* regime, federal courts were displacing substantive state law (torts, property, contracts, etc.).
   - This wasn't working well: harder to regulate behavior, lack of a uniform system within the same jurisdiction.

## Erie Tests

### Step 1: When to Apply?

- Any time there is a conflict between state and federal law (diversity, supplemental jurisdiction, or any other reason state law is before a federal court).
- Substantive → state law. Procedural → federal law.
- How to tell if something is substantive or procedural? Apply the tests below.

### Step 2: Determine Type of Law at Issue

- Constitution; Statute; FRCP; Federal Common Law.

### Step 3: Apply Four *Erie* Tests

- (a) *York* (outcome-determinative)
- (b) *Hanna* Majority — Scalia approach
- (c) *Hanna* Majority — Ginsburg approach
- (d) Harlan Primary Conduct

<!-- page: 9 -->

## The *York* Test

**Rule:** If the choice is outcome-determinative, use state law.

**What does "outcome-determinative" mean?**
- If we use state law, one result would occur. If we use federal law, another result would occur. Therefore, the choice is outcome-determinative.
- The choice determines the outcome of the whole case.
- **Examples:** statute of limitations; rule that requires a supplemental affidavit with the complaint.

**Exception:** *York* addressed only choices that are determinative for the outcome of the entire case.
- Preliminary injunction, class certification — not determinative to the outcome of the entire suit, just one part of the case.

<!-- page: 10 -->

## *Hanna* Majority Test — Constitution

1. **Validity:** Presume that the Constitution is valid.

2. **Applicability:**
   - **Scalia:** Follow the plain textual meaning of the relevant constitutional provision.
   - **Ginsburg:** Although potentially more flexible than Scalia, unlikely to find the constitutional provision inapplicable.

<!-- page: 11 -->

## *Hanna* Majority Test — Federal Statute

1. **Validity**
   - Constitutional provision that gives Congress the power to pass the statute.
   - Common constitutional provisions:
     - Article III, § 2 (gives Congress power to regulate federal courts)
     - Necessary and Proper Clause (gives Congress power to enact housekeeping laws)
     - Commerce Clause (gives Congress power to regulate interstate commerce)

2. **Applicability**
   - **Scalia:** Focus on the plain text of the statute.
     - *Example:* *Stewart v. Ricoh* dissent — no textual support for the proposition that 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) covers forum selection clauses because the statute doesn't say "forum selection clause." As a result, it's inapplicable and we should use state law because of the Rules of Decisions Act (§ 1652).
   - **Ginsburg:** Possible creative interpretation that narrows the federal statute or makes it inapplicable, but still relatively deferential to the statute.

<!-- page: 12 -->

## *Hanna* Majority Test — FRCP

1. **Validity**
   - Rules Enabling Act (28 U.S.C. § 2072) empowers SCOTUS to create the FRCP.
   - FRCP must not "abridge, enlarge, or modify state substantive right." § 2072(b).
   - *Sibbach*: A FRCP does not "abridge, enlarge, or modify" a substantive right if it "really regulates procedure."
   - (Mnemonic: "Rules Enabling Act, *Sibbach*, really regulates procedure.")

2. **Applicability**
   - **Scalia:** Plain text interpretation. *Example:* *Hanna*.
   - **Ginsburg:** Attempt to find creative interpretation that renders federal law inapplicable.
     - *Examples:* *Walker* and *Shady Grove* dissent.
     - Possible ways federal law could be inapplicable:
       1. Federal law is silent on the issue.
       2. Federal law sets the floor/a minimum; state law adds additional requirements.

<!-- page: 13 -->

## *Hanna* Majority Test — Federal Common Law

**Framework:** Apply the Twin Aims of *Erie*:
1. Inequitable administration of the law
2. Forum shopping

**Scalia and Ginsburg approach the issue the same way:** Apply state law when applying federal law would invite forum-shopping and lead to inequitable administration of the law.

**Example:** Assignment 2, 2023
- Federal common law provides that choice-of-law clauses are enforceable.
- Both PA and NY law renders such clauses invalid.
- Using federal common law would lead to outcome-determinative differences, resulting in inequitable administration of the law and forum-shopping → use state law.

<!-- page: 14 -->

## Harlan Primary Conduct Test

- **Issue affects primary conduct** → substantive → state law applies.
- **Issue does not affect primary conduct** → procedural → federal law applies.

**Primary conduct** (pre-litigation conduct; behavior in daily life):
- Where a company decides to incorporate, where an individual buys products, etc.
- *Black & White Taxicab* — reincorporated to try to take advantage of different laws.
- *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.* — state law says that if you want to file a shareholders' derivative lawsuit, you must produce a litigation bond.

**Not primary conduct** (issues that lawyers care about; post-litigation conduct):
- Size of paper.
- Tagging (*Hanna*).

**Ambiguous:**
- Statute of limitations — could possibly impact investment strategies.

## Slide 4: When is Preclusion Implicated?

Look for: lawsuit #1 and then lawsuit #2.

**Steps when you see lawsuit #2:**
- Are we in the same court system? If we have two different jurisdictions → intersystem.
- Can we use claim preclusion?
- Can we use issue preclusion?

**Examples of questions/prompts:**
- How could a party use the result in lawsuit #1 in lawsuit #2?
- What defenses could a party make in lawsuit #2?

**Speaker notes:** General background to preclusion: Preclusion — trying to incentivize parties to litigate things in lawsuit #1 so that we don't need a lawsuit #2. Prevents a litigant who had an obligation to assert a theory of relief in lawsuit #1 that has gone to judgment in the trial court from asserting that theory of relief in a subsequent lawsuit #2. At the same time, we want to be cognizant of due process, and make sure you are not precluded if you weren't a party to the initial lawsuit. The preclusion tests help us balance these interests. Preclusion will likely come up on the exam. The moment you see lawsuit #2, immediately think preclusion. Make sure to do your claim preclusion and your issue preclusion analysis separately. First, ask if there's claim preclusion, go through the elements, do the claim preclusion analysis. THEN ask if there's issue preclusion, go through elements, do the issue preclusion analysis.

- **Step 1:** Same claim
- **Step 2:** Same parties (or parties in privity)
- **Step 3:** Judgment that is **final** and on the **merits**

**Speaker notes:** Same as Slide 4 notes. (General background on preclusion and exam approach — see Slide 4.)

## Slide 6: Claim Preclusion — Step 0 (Stealth Factors)

**Ability to adjudicate claim in lawsuit #1.**
- Court #1 must have had the **ability** to adjudicate the theory of relief that someone is trying to preclude in lawsuit #2.
- Could you have brought this claim in lawsuit #1? Check for SMJ, PJ, and venue to make sure the court in lawsuit #1 could have heard the claim.
- Ex = *Marrese v. Orthopedic Surgeons*: state court in L1 couldn't adjudicate federal antitrust claims.
- Watch out for this with agencies.

**Rendering court limited claim preclusive effect of its decision.**
- "Dismissal without prejudice."

**Speaker notes:** Can think of these as step "zero" — step 0(a) and step 0(b), can check before getting into entire claim preclusion analysis. Ability to adjudicate — if state court could not have exercised SMJ over a Sherman Act claim (because it's exclusive federal jurisdiction), then party will not be precluded from bringing a Sherman Act claim in lawsuit #2. If you could NOT have brought claim in lawsuit #1, due to lack of SMJ, PJ, etc. then a party is NOT precluded from bringing claim in lawsuit #2. Limiting preclusive effect — court in lawsuit #1 reduces the claim preclusive effects of its judgment by dismissing without prejudice. If Court #1 dismissed without prejudice, then you are allowed to refile. NOT precluded. So in summary, if either (1) court #1 could not have heard the claim, or (2) court #1 dismissed without prejudice, then you will NOT be claim precluded. If neither of these factors are present, proceed to claim preclusion test.

## Slide 7: Claim Preclusion — Same Claim

**Same Claim**

- **Test** = same transaction or occurrence (*Heyward Robinson*)
  - Whether the facts are related in time, space, origin, or motivation.
  - Whether they form a convenient trial unit.
  - Whether the parties would have expected the two issues to have been tried together.

**Two exceptions:**

1. **Markets that involve divisible interests ("bond exception").**
   - When we want a market for two things that would require them to be separate and separable.
   - Ex: bond market (*Cromwell v. County of Sac*) or real estate (Prof. Greiner's interpretation of *Gonzales*).

2. **Installment contracts.**
   - Acceleration clause (*Jones v. Morris Plan Bank*) vs. no acceleration clause.

**Speaker notes:** Exception re divisible interests: Two things are purchased in the same T or O, but we want a market for the two things separately (if bonds were not separable, we couldn't have a bond market — would all be claim precluded). Each bond is its own separate claim. Bond market → *Cromwell v. County of Sac*: no claim preclusion even though in privity with other bondholder and there is final judgment, because each bond is its own individual promise of payment. Real estate → Prof. Greiner thinks *Gonzales* got this wrong: was actually not part of the same claim because we want real estate plots to be sold individually. Exception re installment contracts: If the person who needs to make payments does not pay, when you are seeking to collect, must sue for all outstanding payments due at that time (not future payments). Acceleration clause → all payments become immediately due; must sue for everything. No acceleration clause → sue for missed and past due payments only (not future payments).

## Slide 8: Claim Preclusion — Same Party

**Same parties (or parties in privity)**
- Ordinarily means same legal entity.
- *Gonzales* plaintiffs were not in privity (even though many similarities).

**Two types of privity:**

1. **Substantial control** — A nonparty substantially controlled conduct/litigation of parties in lawsuit #1. Ex: single shareholder of a company.
2. **Successor in legal interest** — Ex: if you sell the right to collect on debt.

**Speaker notes:** Example for substantial control: Sole owner of a corporation ("close" corporation). Lawsuit by corporation for damages, and then lawsuit by owner for loss in value of shares. Owner is in privity with the corporation because the owner exercised substantial control (or could have exercised, had a right to exercise). Claim preclusion applies. Successor in interest: Ex = credit card debt; debt purchaser #2 brings lawsuit to collect, then debt purchaser #3 cannot also bring the same lawsuit. Easements can also be successor in interest.

## Slide 9: Claim Preclusion — Final & On the Merits

**Judgment that is (1) final and (2) on the merits**

| | Final? |
|---|---|
| Judgment entered by trial court | Yes |
| Full summary judgment followed by final judgment | Yes |

| | On the merits? |
|---|---|
| SMJ, PJ, venue, etc. | Not on the merits |
| Jury verdicts, dismissal for failure to state a claim, SJ | On the merits |
| Failure to respond to discovery, failure to prosecute, default judgment for failure to appear | On the merits |
| SOL; partial summary judgment | Unsettled — jurisdictions differ |

**Speaker notes:** Final: don't care if case is still on appeal; final only with respect to trial court judgment. On the merits: plaintiff's failure to move lawsuit forward — that is on the merits. Why? We don't want people to be able to refile because we have already given them an opportunity to litigate.

## Slide 11: Issue Preclusion — Same Issue

**Same Issue**
- Issues are fairly narrow and they are factual.
- Can be a mixed question of fact and law (e.g., was conduct negligent).
- Most often arise within an element of a cause of action or defense.

**Speaker notes:** We do not use issue preclusion to preclude relitigation of legal issues (we have stare decisis for that). Ex = in *Cromwell*, holder-in-due-course defense is not the same issue as the issues litigated in lawsuit #1. Does not mean transaction or occurrence — issue is much narrower. You are not obligated to raise all potential issues that you possibly could have in lawsuit #1. Policy rationales: We don't want plaintiff/defendant raising any and all possible issues — not efficient. The parties might not know about a certain issue at the time. Negligence is a good example of an issue, IE was the driver negligent in the car accident — if that's been decided in a former lawsuit, can't relitigate that in a subsequent lawsuit.

---

## Slide 12: Issue Preclusion — Actually Litigated

**Actually Litigated**
- Was the issue something that the parties raised in court and fought about?
- Ex = *Cromwell*.

**Speaker notes:** Issue was raised, submitted for determination, and determined. In *Cromwell*, didn't raise holder-in-due-course defense in lawsuit #1, so cannot issue preclude that in lawsuit #2. There could be the same issue across different causes of action — IE did you own the bond could be the basis of several causes of action.

---

## Slide 13: Issue Preclusion — Necessary to the Decision

**Necessary to the decision**
- The issue must matter for the judgment that the court issues.
- Ex = *Rios v. Davis*, not necessary to the decision.

**General jury verdicts:**

| Court | Rule |
|---|---|
| Federal rule | Cannot issue preclude. (*Russell v. Place*) |
| Some state courts | Allow for issue preclusion. |

**Speaker notes:** *Rios* explanation: Because the jury found that Davis was negligent, he could not recover any money (because of contributory negligence). As a result, the finding that Rios was also negligent was not necessary to the decision — it would not have changed the outcome. General jury verdict: Does not distinguish/specify which grounds were the basis of the verdict. Federal rule: Cannot issue preclude based on general jury verdicts with multiple independent grounds because we don't know which issues were necessary to the decision (*Russell v. Place*). Some state courts: Assumption that the verdict means the court ruled in favor of the winning party on all issues litigated — losing party lost on all issues.

## Slide 14: Issue Preclusion — Mutuality

| Mutuality Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Strict mutuality | Same parties + same procedural posture | *Cromwell* |
| Flexible mutuality | Same parties + different procedural position | *Rios v. Davis* |
| Federal rule | Distinguishes offensive vs. defensive nonmutual issue preclusion; generally frowns upon **offensive**, generally okay with **defensive** | *Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore* |
| No mutuality (New York) | Not an element of issue preclusion | — |

**Speaker notes:** Note — JG will tell you what mutuality requirement a state requires; keep track of this on the exam! Strict mutuality: non-mutual offensive/defensive doesn't apply. Flexible mutuality: non-mutual offensive/defensive doesn't apply. Federal rule: discussed on next slide. No mutuality (NY): not an element — non-mutual offensive/defensive both allowed.

## Slide 15: Issue Preclusion — Offensive vs. Defensive Nonmutual Preclusion

**Offensive**
- Same defendant in both lawsuits.
- When the defendant loses on an issue in lawsuit #1, and plaintiff is trying to use issue preclusion in lawsuit #2.
- **Exception:** when plaintiff in lawsuit #2 could not have joined lawsuit #1.

**Defensive**
- Same plaintiff in both lawsuits.
- When the plaintiff loses on an issue in lawsuit #1, defendant in lawsuit #2 can use issue preclusion.

**Policy rationales:** Incentive structure.

**Speaker notes:** Federal rule: Offensive = same defendant (or in privity) in both lawsuits; defendant lost on an issue in lawsuit #1; plaintiff #2 wants to use issue preclusion in lawsuit #2. Exception: when plaintiff #2 could not have joined lawsuit #1 (federal courts generally do not allow otherwise). Defensive = same plaintiff (or in privity) in both lawsuits; plaintiff lost on an issue in lawsuit #1; defendant #2 can use issue preclusion; federal courts generally allow. Policy rationales: Defensive — incentivizes a plaintiff to sue all applicable defendants in one lawsuit, because if they lose, they won't be able to relitigate the issue against a different defendant (efficient). Offensive — incentivizes the second plaintiff to "sit out" of lawsuit #1 (take a wait-and-see approach), which is not efficient. BUT if plaintiff #2 could not have joined lawsuit #1, courts will allow offensive nonmutual estoppel. Ex = *Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore* (plaintiff could not have joined SEC litigation). Other possible reasons = if plaintiff would have destroyed complete diversity.

## Slide 16: Issue Preclusion — Stealth Rules

**Stealth Rules**
1. Only applies to the losing party (or parties in privity with losing party).
2. Judgment of sufficient quality/finality — judgment entered by a trial court. Partial SJ, followed by settlement and dismissal: make policy arguments for and against.
3. Losing party must have had sufficient opportunity/incentive to litigate.

**Speaker notes:** You only get issue precluded if you LOST on the issue in lawsuit #1. What if there is partial SJ on one issue, then the parties settle and dismiss? For: SJ would rarely be revisited or revised, we want to avoid continuing litigation. Against: SJ are subject to revision at any time, doesn't seem very final. Full/fair opportunity: For example, if discovery rules limited you from bringing in an important document, a party wasn't able to call a key witness — a party must be given one full and fair opportunity to litigate an issue.

## Slide 24: Class Certification — Prerequisites: Numerosity

**Numerosity**
- Class is so numerous that joinder of all members is impractical.
- Below 20 = no; above 40 = "numerosity safezone."

## Slide 25: Class Certification — Prerequisites: Commonality

**Commonality**
- Questions of law or fact common to the class — common litigation **answers** to all of their claims.
- *Walmart* standard: plaintiffs must demonstrate that class members have "suffered the same injury."
- Insufficient to say "all suffered violation of same legal provision (e.g., Title VII)" — must have suffered in the same way.
- HCOL can raise issues for commonality.
- Note: if FRCP 23(b)(3) "Damages" lawsuit, **predominance swallows commonality**.

**Speaker notes:** Are there common litigation answers to all claims? Were the class members all wronged for the same reason, harmed by the same scheme in the same way? *Walmart* standard: high bar — need the same answer, not just the same question. Normally, must point to some specific policy or practice engaging each individual class member; lacking a policy/allowing discretion is not itself a policy. Things to consider: Did proposed members have different injuries? Were their contracts the same? HCOL: federal courts use HCOL rules of the state in which they are sitting (*Klaxon*). If laws of different applicable states differ, that might defeat commonality (different elements, defenses, or damages). *Shutts*: if the forum state wants to apply its own law to all claims in a class action, must have sufficient contacts linking defendant and subject matter to the forum state.

## Slide 26: Class Certification — Prerequisites: Typicality

**Typicality**
- Claims or defenses of the representative parties are typical of the claims or defenses of the class.

**Speaker notes:** Named plaintiffs' theory of relief is sufficiently close to the theory of relief asserted by unnamed class members. Enough congruence between claims. When named plaintiff pushes hard to get a maximum recovery, that will benefit the unnamed class members — incentive alignment.

## Slide 27: Class Certification — Prerequisites: Representativeness

**Representativeness**
- The representative parties will fairly and adequately protect the interests of the class.
- Becomes an issue if there are conflicts of interests among the class members.

**Speaker notes:** Conflict of interest — ex: if some class members want money immediately, and others want it available later. *Amchem* asbestos poisoning case: some plaintiffs were presently sick and wanted immediate relief; other plaintiffs were showing symptoms but weren't sick yet and didn't want to exhaust the money. Representativeness was lacking because interests were not aligned: injured claimants who faced current medical expenses wanted relief, whereas exposure-only claimants wanted an inflation-protected fund for the future.

## Slide 28: Class Types — Injunction (FRCP 23(b)(1)(A); FRCP 23(b)(2))

**In general:** qualifies if defendant's conduct is common to all class members such that an injunction would be an appropriate remedy.

- **23(b)(1)(A):** When a defendant may not be able to comply with conflicting injunctions that could come from a series of individual lawsuits.
- **23(b)(2):** Defendant acted in a way that applied "generally to the class" so it makes sense to give injunctive relief for the class as a whole.

**Notice and opt-out:** Does not require that individual class members be notified or given the opportunity to opt-out at certification. Court "**may**" direct notice per FRCP 23(c)(2)(A).

**Speaker notes:** 23(b)(1)(A): injunctive remedy in lawsuit A could be in opposition to injunctive remedy in lawsuit B, so defendant could not comply with terms of both. 23(b)(2): *Wal-Mart* — this rule does not include actions where each class member is entitled to different remedies (i.e., different injunctive or declaratory relief from the defendant).

## Slide 29: Class Types — Individual Lawsuit Adversity (FRCP 23(b)(1)(B))

**FRCP 23(b)(1)(B)**
- Adjudication of certain individuals' lawsuits could adversely affect other class members.
- Ex: when a limited fund is involved, like an insurance policy — the policy could run out of money before plaintiffs who file later are paid.

**Notice and opt-out:** Does not require notice or opt-out. Court "**may**" direct notice per FRCP 23(c)(2)(A).

**Speaker notes:** Same as Slide 28 notes.

## Slide 30: Class Types — Damages (FRCP 23(b)(3))

**FRCP 23(b)(3)**

If class action is seeking damages, it must meet:
1. **Predominance** — essentially a heightened requirement of commonality; common issues of law and fact must predominate over issues that are not common.
2. **Superiority** — class device must be deemed superior to individual adjudications.

**Notice and opt-out:** Reasonable efforts must be made to give notice; class members must be provided opportunity to opt-out. Court "**must**" provide notice per FRCP 23(c)(2)(b).

**Speaker notes:** Predominance: common issues of law and fact must predominate. Superiority: presumption that individual plaintiffs controlling their own lawsuits is better — but class action is superior when individual claims would return very small damages, when plaintiffs are geographically diverse, etc. Notice and opt-out: requires individual notice (mail or email); must provide opportunity to opt-out.

## Slide 31: Class Actions and SMJ

**AUJ:** AUJ cause of action common to all class members (*Parklane Hosiery*).

**DJ — Complete Diversity:** *Ben Hur* — look at the diversity of only the **named plaintiff** and defendant. Domicile of unnamed class members is irrelevant.

- "Contamination theory."

**Speaker notes:** DJ complete diversity: Court lacks SMJ if named plaintiff and defendant are not completely diverse (*Strawbridge*). Supports concept of class as single entity — we only care about citizenship of the named plaintiff. AIC: Does NOT work for multiple defendants (Rule 20 almost always applies; under 1367(b) ouster, cannot bring claims because destroys requirements of §1332). *Snyder v. Harris*: no stacking AIC in class action — distinct claims in a class action cannot be added together to satisfy AIC requirement. *Exxon* Rule: With a diversity anchoring claim (named plaintiff completely diverse and AIC sufficient), AIC-deficient plaintiffs do not "contaminate" the anchoring claim under 1367(a). Why? Purpose of AIC rule was to ensure the case was significant enough for federal court — that purpose is still met even if unnamed parties are AIC deficient.

## Slide 32: Class Actions and PJ

**General PJ** over defendant: plaintiffs could sue defendant in defendant's home state (i.e., domicile, place of incorporation, or PPOB).

**Specific PJ** over defendant: when claims of only some class members have minimum contacts, can the court exercise PJ over the defendant for claims that lack minimum contacts?

| View | Description | Supporting Cases |
|---|---|---|
| **View 1: Class as simple joinder on steroids** | Apply *Bristol-Myers Squibb*: courts cannot exercise PJ over a defendant with respect to claims that lack minimum contacts. | *Shutts* (cannot treat class as aggregate unit for HCOL purposes) |
| **View 2: Certification creates a new legal entity** | If the class is a single legal entity, it is harmed in many locations (where any plaintiffs suffered injury). **Speaker notes:** For claims with minimum contacts, walk through specific PJ rules (long arm statute, minimal contacts, fairness factors). Resident vs. nonresident class member plaintiffs: question is whether there is PJ over the defendant for claims of nonresident class members that lack minimum contacts. Ex = *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, simple joinder case. View 1: BMS applies to state courts, and Rule 4(k)(1)(A) says federal courts can only exercise PJ when state courts can — federal courts also follow this rule (piggy-back). View 2: If there is a scheme that occurs across different states, could argue defendant perpetrated the scheme in all of the states. Analogous to a trust.

## Part I: Appellate Jurisdiction

### Overview

Appellate jurisdiction is a threshold question: before asking whether you will *win* on appeal, you must ask whether you have the *right* to seek appellate review at all. There are three sections of the U.S. Code that confer appellate jurisdiction: **§ 1291**, **§ 1292**, and **§ 1651**. On an exam, it is useful to organize your answer around these three sections.

---

### § 1291 — Final Judgment Rule

§ 1291 confers appellate jurisdiction in essentially three circumstances, all involving some form of final disposition.

#### Category 1: The Whole Case Is Over

The most straightforward case: the trial court has entered final judgment and everyone has gone home. This is quintessential § 1291 jurisdiction — yes, you can appeal.

#### Category 2: Final Judgment as to One Claim (*Sears*)

If a case has multiple claims and the trial court dismisses one while the others remain pending, you may still have appellate jurisdiction over the dismissed claim. The test from *Sears* <!-- corrected: "Sears" → *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Mackey* --> asks:

1. Has the court **explicitly entered judgment** with respect to that particular claim? and
2. Has the court **explicitly determined there is no just reason for delay**?

On an exam, Professor Greiner <!-- corrected: "Griner"/"grounder" → "Greiner" throughout --> will typically tell you that the court entered final judgment on a claim and ask whether immediate appeal is available. The "no just reason for delay" factor does not do much work in most exam contexts, but you should mention it.

> **Example of a "just reason for delay":** Suppose a trial court grants judgment as a matter of law (directed verdict) on one claim mid-trial while the jury is still deliberating on two other claims. An appellate court might say: the jury will reach its verdict within days — just do it all at once. That could constitute a just reason for delay.

This category also implicates **Rule 54(b)**: § 1291 is the source of jurisdiction, but Rule 54(b) is what allows the court to enter final judgment on fewer than all claims.

#### Category 3: The *Cohen* Exception (Collateral Order Doctrine)

The *Cohen* exception <!-- corrected: *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.* --> allows interlocutory appeal of orders that are not final dispositions of the whole case or of a particular claim, but rather of *collateral* issues — issues severable from the merits.

**Four elements (all required):**

1. **Final disposition of a claimed right** — there has been a definitive ruling on an issue. In *Cohen*, the court ordered the plaintiff to post a litigation bond: a final ruling on whether the bond was required.

2. **Collateral to the merits** — the issue must be completely severable from the underlying claims. Whether a party must post a litigation bond has nothing to do with whether they will prevail on the merits of the lawsuit.

3. **Unreviewable on later appeal** — this is the factor that does the most work, analogous to irreparable harm in the preliminary injunction context. In *Cohen*, once a party has been ordered to buy a litigation bond and complies, a later appellate ruling that the bond was not required comes too late — the bond has already been purchased. Courts almost always invoke this factor to *deny* interlocutory review.

4. **Stealth factor (yes-or-no legal question)** — the issue must be a clean yes-or-no legal question, not a request for the appellate court to interpret a statute or resolve a factual dispute. "Do I have to post a litigation bond?" is yes-or-no. "What does this statute mean?" is not.

> **Note on class certification:** Denial of class certification is sometimes allowed interlocutory review despite not clearly satisfying all *Cohen* factors, because class certification is so important and case-dispositive in practice. This is an example of courts treating the doctrine flexibly.

---

### § 1292 — Interlocutory Appeals

§ 1292 provides two routes to interlocutory appeal. This is easier than § 1291.

#### § 1292(a)(1) — Injunctions

Any order pertaining to an injunction — creating, dissolving, or modifying one — is immediately appealable. Note: *denials* of injunctions may not qualify under this provision (Professor Greiner has flagged this in class).

#### § 1292(b) — Double Discretion (Certified Questions)

The trial court may certify a question for immediate appellate review. This is called "double discretion" because it is discretionary at both levels:

- **First discretion:** The trial court judge decides whether to certify the issue. There is no obligation to do so.
- **Second discretion:** After certification, the appellate court decides whether to take up the question.

**Three factors courts consider for certification:**

1. **Controlling question of law** — will this question determine the outcome of the case or a significant part of it?
2. **Substantial ground for differing views** — is this a genuinely contested legal question on which reasonable courts disagree?
3. **Material advancement of the litigation** — will resolving the issue now save significant time and expense?

These factors overlap and are fairly abstract; they give judges broad discretion. On an exam, go through all three.

> **Example:** In *Trump v. United States*, <!-- corrected: *Trump v. United States* --> the question of presidential immunity at the district court level was a classic candidate for § 1292(b) certification: controlling question of law, substantial ground for differing views (the D.C. Circuit and Supreme Court ultimately disagreed), and would materially advance the litigation. As it happened, the court did not certify; the D.C. Circuit took the case up anyway given its constitutional importance.

**Exam tips:**
- Certification must be **in writing** — if the facts say the judge certified orally, that is a defect worth noting (Professor Greiner has given bonus points for catching this).
- After certification, the party has **10 days** to file an application for appeal with the appellate court. Missing this deadline forfeits the right.

---

### § 1651 — Mandamus

Mandamus almost never succeeds. Think of it as the last resort — asking to speak to the manager. It is available only when no other route to appellate review exists.

There are two flavors within § 1651:

**Irreparable harm (emergency mandamus):** The party must show:
1. There is an **emergency** — irreparable harm is imminent, and the appellate court must intervene now.
2. The party is **clearly and indisputably correct** on the law — not just probably right; obviously right.
3. **Consider other parties and the government's interest** — Professor Greiner has described this as essentially asking: would it be a good idea? This factor is highly discretionary.

**Supervisory writ:** Granted when the trial court judge is misbehaving — potentially requiring a pattern of misbehavior. The *La Buy* case <!-- corrected: "Labai case" → *La Buy v. Howes Leather Co.* --> is an example: the judge was delegating all judicial work to a special master, far outside the bounds of proper judicial conduct. For supervisory writs, consider the *Cheney* factors <!-- corrected: "cheney" → *Cheney v. United States District Court* --> in addition to the standard mandamus analysis.

> **Note:** The policy animating §§ 1292(b) and 1651 is the same: appellate courts should not be flooded with piecemeal appeals. Both routes are reserved for exceptional circumstances.

## Part II: Multi-Party Litigation and Supplemental Jurisdiction

### Why This Matters on the Exam

Multi-party litigation is one of the most heavily tested topics in this course. It is complex in its own right and implicates subject matter jurisdiction, personal jurisdiction, venue, and preclusion all at once. On an exam with multiple plaintiffs, multiple defendants, and third-party defendants who themselves have claims against the plaintiff, you must **draw diagrams** — if you do not, you will get lost.

### Four Dimensions to Track on Every Exam

As you read the facts, note:
1. **Choice of law** — which state's law applies to each issue?
2. **Venue / transfer** — where was the case filed, and has it been transferred?
3. **Multi-party litigation** — who is suing whom, under what joinder rule?
4. **Preclusion** — where was any prior lawsuit, and which preclusion rules apply?

### Core Principles

**Every claim in a multi-party case requires its own independent analysis** of subject matter jurisdiction, personal jurisdiction, and venue. The fact that the court has personal jurisdiction over a defendant as to one claim does not mean it has personal jurisdiction as to every claim against that defendant.

**The same transaction or occurrence standard is very broad.** The standard articulated in *Hayward v. Robinson* is that two claims arise from the same transaction or occurrence when there is a logical relationship between them. Professor Greiner consistently emphasizes that this standard almost always will be satisfied — do not spend much time on it on the exam; just identify something and move on.

**Compulsory claims:** If a claim is compulsory and you fail to bring it, you are barred from bringing it later. Note: Rule 19 (compulsory joinder of parties) is not covered in this course. What *is* covered is the compulsory counterclaim rule (Rule 13(a)).

---

### Joinder Rules

#### Rule 13 — Counterclaims and Cross-Claims

Rule 13 applies to all counterclaims, including counterclaims by plaintiffs. For example, if a third-party defendant (TPD) files a claim against the original plaintiff, and the plaintiff wishes to counterclaim against TPD, Rule 13 applies to that counterclaim just as it does to a defendant's counterclaim against the plaintiff.

#### Rule 14 — Impleader (Third-Party Defendants)

A defendant may implead a third-party defendant if doing so shifts liability from the defendant to that third party. Classic examples: impleading an insurer, or a raw materials supplier who sold defective components.

#### Rule 20 — Permissive Joinder

Rule 20 is the primary joinder rule. Two requirements:
1. Claims arise from the **same transaction or occurrence** (almost always satisfied).
2. There is a **common question of law or fact** (Professor Greiner says this always exists; just identify something).

**Critical point:** When you add a second defendant to a case, *both* defendants are joined via Rule 20 — not just the newly added one. This has significant consequences for the § 1367(b) analysis (see below).

#### Kitchen Sink Rule (Rule 18)

Once a party has been properly joined to the case, the plaintiff may bring *any* other claim against that party, even if that claim does not arise from the same transaction or occurrence. This is different from Rule 20 joinder.

---

### Supplemental Jurisdiction — § 1367

**§ 1367(a):** If a court has original jurisdiction over one claim (the "anchoring claim"), it may exercise supplemental jurisdiction over all other claims that are part of the same case — i.e., share a common nucleus of operative fact (*Gibbs* <!-- corrected: *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs* -->).

**§ 1367(b):** Limits supplemental jurisdiction when the anchoring claim's only basis for original jurisdiction is **diversity** (§ 1332). Specifically, § 1367(b) bars supplemental jurisdiction over:

- Claims **by plaintiffs** against parties joined via **Rules 14, 19, 20, or 24**, when those claims would be inconsistent with § 1332 (i.e., would destroy complete diversity or fail the amount-in-controversy requirement); and
- Claims **by plaintiffs joined via Rules 19 or 24** against any defendant, when those claims would be inconsistent with § 1332.

**Key rule:** § 1367(b) only applies to **plaintiffs**. Claims by defendants — including third-party defendants' claims against original plaintiffs — are not subject to § 1367(b). A third-party defendant who is not diverse from the original plaintiff can still sue that plaintiff via supplemental jurisdiction, because TPD is a defendant.

However, if the *plaintiff* wishes to counterclaim against a non-diverse TPD, that counterclaim is a claim *by a plaintiff*, and § 1367(b) applies. Since the parties are not diverse and there is no arising-under jurisdiction, that counterclaim would be dismissed for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.

---

### Contamination Theory (*Exxon Mobil*)

<!-- corrected: "exon" → *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services* throughout -->

This is one of the more counterintuitive rules in the course and a favorite exam topic.

**The apparent gap in § 1367(b):** Consider a case with P1 (NY) suing D (CA) for $100,000 — a valid diversity claim. P2 (CA) then joins via Rule 20 with a claim against D (CA) for $50,000. By the literal text of § 1367(b):

- § 1367(b) bars claims *by plaintiffs* against defendants joined via *Rules 14, 19, 20, or 24* — but D here is **not** joined via any of those rules; D was already the defendant.
- § 1367(b) also bars claims by plaintiffs joined via Rules *19 or 24* — but P2 joined via **Rule 20**, not 19 or 24.

So by the text, § 1367(b) would not oust P2's claim against D, even though P2 and D are not diverse. This appears to be a drafting error, which the Supreme Court acknowledged in *Exxon Mobil*.

**Contamination theory (the *Exxon Mobil* holding):** Even though § 1367(b) does not textually apply, the Supreme Court held that P2's non-diverse claim is still ousted — through **§ 1367(a)**, not § 1367(b). The theory (which the TA describes as a legal fiction): P2's claim, by destroying complete diversity between the parties, "contaminates" the original jurisdiction the court has over the anchoring claim (P1 v. D). Without that original jurisdiction, there is no anchoring claim, and therefore no supplemental jurisdiction for P2's claim to attach to.

**On an exam:** If you oust this claim via § 1367(b), you get no credit. You must identify it as ousted via § 1367(a) and contamination theory.

**Critical distinction — amount in controversy vs. diversity:**

*Exxon Mobil* draws a sharp line:

| P2's deficiency | Result |
|----------------|--------|
| Not diverse from D (same state) | Claim is **ousted** via contamination theory |
| Diverse from D, but AIC < $75,000 | Claim is **allowed** (AIC deficiency does not trigger contamination) |

The Court's justification: diversity is about fairness (contaminated by non-diversity); amount in controversy is about importance (the importance of the first claim is not diminished by the second claim's low value).

**The second-defendant twist:** This is where things get even more complex. In the single-defendant scenario above, P2's claim with an AIC deficiency *is* allowed. But the moment you add a second defendant (D2), **both D1 and D2 are joined via Rule 20**. Now P2's claim against D1 or D2 is a claim by a plaintiff against a party joined via Rule 20 — and § 1367(b) *does* apply. Under § 1367(b), an AIC deficiency is just as problematic as a diversity deficiency. So once there are two defendants, the Exxon AIC exception disappears.

**Class actions (exception to contamination):** In class actions, *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble* <!-- corrected: "supreme tribe of ben hur" → *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble* --> holds that only the **named plaintiffs** must be diverse from the defendants and satisfy amount in controversy. Unnamed class members can get in via supplemental jurisdiction even if they would break diversity or fail AIC. Contamination theory does not apply in class actions.

---

### Practical Exam Guidance for Multi-Party Litigation

When drawing out a multi-party diagram, for each arrow (i.e., each claim), track:

1. **Subject matter jurisdiction** — arising under (§ 1331), diversity (§ 1332), or supplemental (§ 1367)?
2. **Joinder rule** — how did this party enter the case (Rule 13, 14, 20, 24)?
3. **§ 1367(b) applicability** — is this a claim by a plaintiff, and is the anchor diversity-only?
4. **Personal jurisdiction** — specific or general; analyzed claim-by-claim.
5. **Domicile and AIC** — note these as you go; you may need them for supplemental jurisdiction analysis.

Sometimes the anchoring claim's basis for jurisdiction will be unclear (could be arising under, could be diversity). In that case, work through both possibilities: "If the anchoring claim is arising under, then... If the anchoring claim is diversity only, then..."

## Part III: Intervention (Rule 24)

Intervention is relatively straightforward. The test comes from *Smuck v. Hobson*. <!-- corrected: "Smock" → *Smuck v. Hobson* -->

**Three required elements:**

1. **The would-be intervenor has an interest** in the subject matter of the litigation.
2. **The outcome of the case could affect that interest** — the litigation could as a practical matter impair the intervenor's ability to protect that interest.
3. **The interest is not adequately represented** by the existing parties — i.e., the current parties are not sufficiently incentivized to protect the intervenor's interests. If existing parties already have aligned interests, intervention may be denied.

Cite *Smuck* and work through these elements. They are treated as requirements, not merely factors.

> **Note:** Every new claim added through intervention also requires its own subject matter jurisdiction, personal jurisdiction, and venue analysis.

## Part IV: Selected Q&A

### On *Cohen* Elements

**Q: Do you need all four Cohen factors?**
Yes — the stealth factor and all others must be satisfied.

**Q: Can you give more examples of "final disposition of a claimed right"?**
*Cohen* itself is the cleanest example: the court's order that the plaintiff *must* post a litigation bond is a final disposition of the plaintiff's claimed right *not* to post the bond. The cases on the slides illustrate both where the exception applies and where it does not.

### On § 1292(b) Double Discretion

**Q: Who would certify a question for interlocutory review?**
Either party can ask the trial judge to certify. The judge has discretion to certify sua sponte or at a party's request.

**Q: On an exam, how do you handle § 1292(b) when there's no indication the judge certified anything?**
Say: "There is no evidence the judge certified this question for immediate review under § 1292(b), but if she had — or if we wanted to argue she should — the factors would be..." and then work through the three factors.

**Q: Is the risk of flooding appellate courts a *Cohen* stealth factor?**
You can think of it that way. It is a policy consideration that cuts across all three routes to appellate jurisdiction and helps explain why interlocutory appeals are generally disfavored.

### On *Shaffer v. Heitner* and In Rem Jurisdiction

**Q: Does *Shaffer v. Heitner* <!-- corrected: "Schaefer's Heitner" → *Shaffer v. Heitner* --> apply to in rem jurisdiction?**

*Shaffer* held that all assertions of personal jurisdiction must satisfy minimum contacts. *Burnham v. Superior Court* <!-- corrected: *Burnham v. Superior Court* --> later suggested that at least tag jurisdiction (physical presence) may not require a minimum contacts analysis, casting doubt on whether *Shaffer*'s "all" means all.

For in rem (as opposed to quasi in rem): yes, *Shaffer* technically applies, but minimum contacts will almost always be satisfied. If you are litigating title to land, the land has contacts with the forum state by definition, and the subject matter of the litigation — settling title — is inherently connected to the forum. So the answer is yes, minimum contacts is required, but it will virtually always exist in the in rem context.

### On FRCP 4 and Electronic Service

FRCP 4 is best understood as incorporating state law rules on service of process into the federal rules, and also providing independent federal procedures for service. Electronic service is generally disfavored. The analysis of notice should center on *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.* <!-- corrected: "malayne" → *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.* --> and the "reasonably calculated to provide notice" standard. You do not need to go deep into FRCP 4 on an exam.

### On Switching Anchoring Claims

**Q: Can you switch your anchoring claim mid-lawsuit? E.g., if P1's claim against D1 was diversity-based, but later P2 joins with an arising-under claim against D1 — can that become the new anchor?**

Yes. Courts evaluate subject matter jurisdiction on an ongoing basis and must find it wherever it exists. If a valid arising-under claim is added to the case, all other claims can potentially anchor to it via § 1367(a) — and § 1367(b) will not apply, because § 1367(b) is only triggered when the anchoring claim's sole basis is diversity.
