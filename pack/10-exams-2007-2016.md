## Question 1 (65 points)

Assume that Alabama state law invalidates contractual forum-selection clauses, but that New York law nearly always enforces them. For federal law on the subject, remember Kennedy and O'Connor's concurrence in *Stewart v. Ricoh*. Assume also that Alabama's long-arm statute provides, "Alabama courts may exercise personal jurisdiction over any and all parties when such exercise would be consistent with the Constitution of the United States. Process may be served on any party in any manner consistent with the Constitution of the United States." Assume Alabama still follows the First Restatement regarding choice of law. [By the way, in real life, all of this may be untrue. This is an exam question only. Same goes for the rest of the exam.]

In May, 2006, software engineer Peter Parker, a resident and domiciliary of Mississippi, signed a contract to work for Excellent Software, Inc. ("ESI"). Parker's employment contract had a three-year term at a salary of $150,000.00 per year. The contract included the following boilerplate clause: "Any lawsuit by Employee [meaning Parker] against ESI relating in any way to this contract shall take place in a court located in the jurisdiction of ESI's principal place of business. Any lawsuit against any ESI shareholder relating in any way to this contract shall take place in a court located in the jurisdiction of that shareholder's domicile (if the shareholder is a human being) or in the place of its incorporation (if the shareholder is a corporation)."

At all times relevant, 30% of the voting stock of ESI was owned by the Dong Ah Corporation [no, not the same Dong Ah from the *Thane-Coate* case; I just reused the name], an entity incorporated in the People's Republic of China (and itself wholly owned by another Chinese entity with ties to the Chinese government). Dong Ah's principal place of business was in Albany, New York, where Dong Ah operated a large software facility.

In May of 2006, ESI was a Mississippi corporation with its principal place of business in Mississippisville, a few miles on the Mississippi side of the Mississippi/Alabama border. In June of 2006, however, ESI's chief executive officer, John Boss, proposed to move ESI's headquarters (*i.e.*, principal place of business) to Alabama to take advantage of favorable tax laws. The move required the approval of the majority of the stockholders; Dong Ah voted its shares against the move, but as Dong Ah had only 30% of the shares, it lost the vote, and the move to Alabama proceeded. ESI and all 30 of its employees began operations in Alabamasville, Alabama, a small town just on the Alabama side of the Mississippi/Alabama border. The move was completed by December of 2006, and at that time, Parker began to travel to Alabamasville each day to work.

<!-- page: 2 -->

In February of 2007, Parker traveled with Boss to inspect the Dong Ah facility in Albany. At that time, ESI and Dong Ah were in negotiations to merge; all merger negotiations were taking place in Albany. During this trip, Dong Ah's regional head of operations, Chenxin Zhang, asked Boss to step into a private conference room. Once inside, Zhang told Boss that the merger could not go forward unless ESI fired Parker. When asked for a reason, Zhang replied that Dong Ah had discovered via the Internet that Parker was writing certain software in his spare time that might compete with software Dong Ah was developing, and that "it would be best if he [Parker] didn't find out about what we're doing, which he could if we merged. The people back in China who own my company ordered me to say that you had to get rid of him." Fifteen minutes after this meeting was over, Boss called Parker into the same conference room and fired Parker.

Parker sued ESI and Dong Ah in Alabama state court on April 9, 2007. Against ESI, the claim was state law breach of contract; against Dong Ah, the claim was tortious interference with contractual relations[^1] under state law (refer to this litigation as the "First Case"). Against Dong Ah, Parker requested compensatory and punitive damages totaling $500,000.00. On April 16, an Alabama marshal served a copy of Parker's complaint upon a low-level Dong Ah software engineer who happened to be in Alabamasville inspecting ESI's facility there as part of the merger negotiations. This employee took a copy of the lawsuit back to Albany. On the same day, the marshal served ESI at its Alabamasville location.

On April 17, Parker and ESI settled, and accordingly, on April 23, Parker filed a stipulation dismissing ESI from the Alabama state court litigation.

On April 24, Dong Ah removed the lawsuit to Alabama federal court and on the next day moved "to transfer this case under 28 U.S.C. §§ 1404(a) and 1406(a)" to the Federal District Court for the Northern District of New York (where Albany is located). Over Parker's opposition, Alabama federal judge G. Goblin granted the motion, stating, "This case belongs in New York. Important events happened there. Plus, my docket is really crowded. I don't care which transfer statute applies; the New York court can figure that out." The transfer order was signed in late May, 2007, and the record of the First Case arrived in New York federal district court and was assigned to Judge Mary J. Watson.

On June 4, Parker sued Dong Ah in the Supreme Court of New York (New York's state trial-level court), Albany division, again alleging tortious interference with contractual relations (call this case the "Second Case"). On June 11, Dong Ah removed the Second Case, citing diversity of citizenship, to the Federal District Court for the Northern District of New York. Dong Ah's request to have this case also assigned to Judge Watson was granted the next day.

Shortly thereafter, Dong Ah filed motions to dismiss both cases, in favor of litigation in the People's Republic of China, citing the contractual forum selection clause. Parker opposed, and both parties filed briefs. Judge Watson scheduled a hearing on the motions for August 1, 2007. At the hearing, in addition to arguing against Dong Ah's motions, Parker's attorney

<!-- page: 3 -->

moved to remand the Second Case, saying it had been improperly removed. Dong Ah opposed the remand motion.

On August 2, Judge Watson issued an order remanding the Second Case to the Supreme Court of New York, Albany Division. In the First Case, she issued an order stating:

> I find that the contractual forum selection clause is enforceable. I will not dismiss on this basis, however, because I am worried that Dong Ah will attempt to avoid the merits of dispute in the court system of the People's Republic. Accordingly, I hereby STAY this case. This stay means that the case will remain on my list of active cases, that I will not enter an order of dismissal yet, and I am retaining jurisdiction in case Dong Ah attempts to do any of the following in litigation in the People's Republic of China: plead a statute of limitations defense; avoid any reasonable discovery Parker might request; or take any other action to prevent the court in the People's Republic from adjudicating the merits of this dispute. Should Parker convince me that Dong Ah has in fact done any of the above, I will lift the stay and proceed with this litigation. Otherwise, once a court in the People's Republic adjudicates the merits, I will dismiss the Second Case with prejudice.
>
> It seems like this case would benefit from Second Circuit review; my ruling today is probably controlling with respect to the case, and an appeal would materially advance its ultimate disposition. There is certainly no just reason to delay anything. Anyway, I'm pretty sure the Second Circuit would affirm. This case is a no-brainer.

You are a low-level attorney in the office of Dong Ah's general counsel. Your boss has just received a telephone call from Parker's attorney relating that Parker intends to try to get review of the stay in the First Case in the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. Your boss would herself like to get review of Judge Watson's remand decision in the Second Case. Your boss asks for a memorandum addressing the following three (a, b, and c) issues:

### Question 1(a). With respect to the Second Case,

**(i) (5 points)** can we get review of Judge Watson's order in the Second Circuit? Why or why not?

**(ii) (5 points)** if we could get review, is the order correct? Why or why not?

### Question 1(b). With respect to the First Case,

**(i) (10 points)** can Parker get review of Judge Watson's stay order in the Second Circuit? What possible bases are there, and which might work?

**(ii) (30 points)** if Parker can get review of Judge Watson's stay order, should the Second Circuit affirm or reverse or what? Analyze in full.

<!-- page: 4 -->

### Question 1(c) (15 points).

[For this subpart, pretend that the Second Case goes back to state court, *i.e.*, that the remand is effectuated.]: Can we make any use of the stay order in the First Case to help us get rid of the Second Case? Explain.

**Note:** If you think the Second Circuit might remand the case for factual findings, discuss what facts the Circuit might order the district court to find/consider.

## 2007-final · Instructor model answer

Final Exam Question 1 Model Answer
Question 1(a)
      (i)   The order remanding the Second Case is not appealable.  Period.
 The end.  28 U.S.C. § 1447((d).  Not even mandamus will lie (section 1447
says remand orders are "not reviewable on appeal or otherwise . . .").
[Note to students:  This answer may have been accurate when written, but is
questionable now, after Carlsbad; the remand here could not have been for
lack of SMJ, as there is diversity jurisdiction, bur rather for the defect
in the removal proceeding, see immediately below.]
      (ii)  If we could get the Second Circuit to hear us, we would likely
succeed in getting the remand order reversed.  Our removal was not itself
proper.  Dong Ah has its principal place of business in New York and is
thus a New York citizen for diversity purposes.  Thus, under section
1441(b), our removal was improper because we were sued in our home state
court.  However, under section 1447(c), Parker had only 30 days to move to
remand on the basis of a defect other than lack of subject matter
jurisdiction.  Since Parker moved to remand more than 30 days after our
removal, the only relevant question is whether Parker could have sued us in
New York federal court originally.  He could have.  Parker is an Alabama
citizen, and Dong Ah is New York/PRC.  The amount in controversy obviously
exceeds $75K.  Diversity jurisdiction exists under section 1332.
Question 1(b)
      (i)   Judge Watson's order is likely appealable under the collateral
order doctrine.  The stay here is essentially the same as the stay in Clark
v. Lacy, that is, a stay that awaits the result of another piece of
litigation before the court dismisses.  Judge Watson's order (1)
conclusively resolves the question of whether the FSC is enforceable, and
accordingly, whether the case will go forward in the PRC (unless we screw
up somehow by not complying with her conditions), (2) resolves an important
question (whether the case can go forward in the U.S. at all) that is
independent of the merits of whether we tortiously interfered with Parker's
employment contract, and (3) is effectively otherwise unreviewable on
appeal, since Judge Watson intends to dismiss once a PRC court goes to
final judgment.  Under Cohen, then, the order is probably appealable.
      Other grounds for appeal, if they were needed, probably wouldn't
work.  Judge Watson's stay order is not a final order under 1291; it is
stay, not a dismissal.  There is only one claim, so Rule 54(b) is out, plus
even though Judge Watson said some of Rule 54(b)'s magic words ("no just
reason for delay"), she didn't direct the entry of judgment.  There is no
injunction at issue, 1292(a)(1) is out.  Regarding 1292(b), Judge Watson
did find that her FSC ruling involved a controlling question of law and
that an ultimate appeal might materially advance the termination of the
litigation, but she expressly found that there was NOT substantial ground
for a difference of opinion ("no-brainer").  A petition for a writ of
mandamus might work if Parker could convince the Second Circuit that
something really horrible is going on, but there is no suggestion that
district courts are improperly enforcing FSC clauses right and left.  Plus,
Justices Kennedy and O'Conner think they are fantastic, so a supervisory
writ (La Buy) is unlikely.
      (ii)  If Parker succeeded in getting there, the Second Circuit needs
to decide several items.  First, it needs to decide what the question is:
the enforceability of the FSC, or (less likely) forum non conveniens
(remember Stewart v. Ricoh:  sometimes the choice of question is more
important than the answer).
      Assume for the moment that the issue is whether the FSC is valid and
enforceable, which I think is by far the most likely.  To decide that, it
needs to decide what law governs the issue:  federal common law or state
law.  (Note that it's federal common law that is at issue, not federal
statutory law or a fed civ pro rule; 1404(a) and 1406(a) don't speak to
transfer/dismissal-in-favor-of a court in another country).  That's an Erie
question.  Initially, part of the problem here is that we don't exactly
know what federal law is with respect to the enforceability of FSCs.
Justices Kennedy and O'Conner in the Stewart concurrence said FSCs should
generally be enforced, so I'll assume their view represents federal law.
That means there could be a conflict between federal common law and Alabama
law, since Alabama wouldn't enforce the FSC.  Note that even if federal law
would not enforce the FSC in this case, it seems as though New York law
would, so there could still be a conflict between federal law and New York
law.  (See below re which state's law would apply here.)
      So, getting back to Erie, perhaps the most important thing is that
there is no statute (or rule of civil procedure) that governs the issue of
the enforceability of FSCs (indeed, the FNC doctrine is itself judge-made).
 That means we're NOT in a Hannah situation; rather, we're contemplating
using judge-made law, which we generally don't do (Erie overruled Tyson),
but sometimes we do (e.g., Semtek).  (Note that neither 1404 or 1406 speaks
to situations in which the would-be transferor court is abroad.)  With this
in mind, the Supreme Court has analyzed Erie questions in a lot of
different ways.  If we follow York, we can say that the FSC's
enforceability probably is dispositive of the case, but it may not be
despositive in the sense that York meant.  York involved a statute of
limitations dismissal, which is a legal end to the case.  Here, the FSC
issue may be outcome determinative because (putting aside the justice
available in the PRC court system) the economic incentives are probably not
high enough for Parker to pursue a claim abroad.  That's a lot like arguing
that an FSC ruling sending the case to the PRC is the economic "death
knell" to the litigation, though, and the courts rejected the death knell
doctrine in the context of appealability of class action orders (prior to
the adoption of Rule 23(f)).
      We could also discuss this issue in terms of what several cases
(e.g., Stewart) called the twin aims of Erie.  Plaintiffs will forum shop
to avoid FSCs, and Alabama defendants sued by Alabama plaintiffs on Alabama
causes of action will be treated differently (unequally?) from Alabama
defendants sued by non-Alabama plaintiffs or from non-Alabama defendants
sued by Alabama plaintiffs (either sued on Alabama causes of action).  I
also think that the FSCs like these are likely to affect employers'
"primary conduct" (see Justice Harlan's concurrence in Hannah) in that if
employers know that they will be sued only in their home courts, they might
be more likely to fire U.S. employees, particularly if their home courts
are abroad.  For the same reason, foreign investors might be more willing
to buy stock.  On the whole, then, I think it likely that the Second
Circuit would hold that a state law should govern this issue, although
there's certainly room for debate.  By the way, if I'm wrong about that,
i.e., if federal law should govern, then the Second Circuit might well
affirm.  Kennedy and O'Connoer's concurrence in Stewart lauds FSCs, and the
federal judiciary is generally pro-contract.  The feds would probably say
that Parker knew (or should have known) when he signed his employment
contract that his choice of forum would be limited.
      Now, supposing state law governs, which state?  The case was
originally filed in Alabama and removed there.  It was then transferred to
New York.  If it was transferred under 1404(a), the New York federal court
has to apply Alabama law, including Alabama choice of law rules.  If the
case was transferred under 1406(a), the New York federal court has to apply
New York choice of law rules.  The Judge Goblin didn't specify, so the
Second Circuit will have to decide for itself which statute applies (or
remand directing the district court to decide the issue).  That issue turns
on whether the Alabama court had in personam jurisdiction over Dong Ah.  If
it did, we do 1404(a).  If it didn't, we do 1406(a).  (Note that the
federal venue statute is not applicable because the case was originally
filed in state court and removed.)
      Can an Alabama (with its to-the-maximum-DPC-extent long-arm statute)
constitutionally exercise IPJ over Dong Ah?  Minimum contacts,
International Shoe.  Here, Dong Ah owned stock in what became an Alabama
company, but ESI became an Alabama company over its objection, so this is
sort of like Hanson v. Deckla plus.  Another contact more directly related
to the litigation is Dong Ah's merger talks with ESI, an Alabama company.
There's no information about whether the merger ever went through, however.
 We might argue that it's hard to see how Dong Ah could reasonably have
anticipated getting sued in Alabama, since there's no evidence that it does
any business there.  But, Parker might argue that Dong Ah committed a wrong
that it knew would have effects in Alabama (the lost job), see Kulko,
although the damages (lost wages) are arguably Alabama/Mississippi.
Finally, Parker tagged Dong Ah by serving process on a Dong Ah employee
when that employee was in Alabama on activities related to the proposed
merger (remember that the proposed merger was the impetus for the allegedly
tortious activity).  That's important for minimum contacts, and it might
constitute an entirely independent reason to support IPJ.  See Burnam
plurality.  Yes, the tagged employee was a low-level, but that didn't seem
to bother the court in International Shoe.
      Assume for the moment that the Second Circuit would find that an
Alabama court had IPJ over Dong Ah on whatever theory, then it should
require that Judge Watson use Alabama choice of law rules for the case.
Here, we have another substance versus procedure problem (note that the
substance/procedure line for vertical (Erie) choice of law may be in a
different place form the substance/procedure line in the between-state
context).  Every state "chooses" to use its own procedural law, so if
Alabama would consider the enforceability of the FSC to be a matter of
procedure, it would employ its own law voiding the clause.  We'd need to
research Alabama law here; if there isn't anything directly on point, we
might research how Alabama treats other quasi-procedural issues, such as
statutes of limitations.  Absent any other information, my rank speculation
is that Alabama would consider this a matter of procedure, since the issue
can be decided early in the litigation and it's separate from the "merits"
of whether a tort was committed.  If my rank speculation is correct, the
Second Circuit will reverse and order Judge Watson to proceed with the
case.
      If Alabama would consider the FCS's enforceability a matter of
"substance," then it would conduct a choice of law analysis under the First
Restatement.  The law of the state in which the last event necessary to
"complete" the tort would govern essentially all issues, including the
enforceability of the FSC.  That would probably be New York, since the
firing took place in New York.  One might argue that damages are necessary
to "complete" the tort, and that the damages were suffered in Alabama, but
this is less likely.  So, if as a matter of between-state choice of law,
the FSC's enforceability is considered "substantive," then New York law
would probably apply, and under New York law, the FSC might well be
enforceable.  In that event, the Second Circuit would affirm.
      Now, assume that the Second Circuit finds that an Alabama court could
NOT exercise IPJ over Dong Ah.  In that event, New York state choice of law
rules would apply.  Initially, we'd face the same substance/procedure
question we faced when speaking of Alabama choice of law rules, and we have
no more information here than we did there.  If New York would consider FSC
enforceability procedural, it will apply its own law, and accordingly, the
Second Circuit should affirm.  If New York would considers the FSC
substantive, the Second Circuit will conduct a New York choice of law
analysis.  Under Babcock and the most significant relationship test, the
question is which state has the most significant relationship to the issue
of the FSC's enforceability (as opposed to the whole case).  That might
well be Alabama, or even Mississippi.  The contract was presumably signed
in Mississippi, and at the time it concerned a Mississippi corporation.
Now, the corporation is in Alabama, and Parker works there.  It's hard to
see how New York has much relationship to or interest in the issue of the
enforceability of a contract clause between a person who works in Alabama
and his Alabama employer as relates to an internationally-based
stockholder.  This is like Babcock in that although the tort occurred in
New York, the issue here is one of relationships among the parties.  So New
York choice of law rules might well mandate the application of Alabama
substantive law, in which case the Second Circuit would reverse and order
Judge Watson to proceed to litigate.
      What about if the question is FNC, not the FSC's enforceability?  In
that case, the FSC should be just one among several factors.  We'd have to
go through the same federal versus state, New York versus Alabama choice of
law question to figure out which FNC law applies.  We don't know anything
about the FNC law of New York or Alabama, but their FNC doctrines are
anything like the federal doctrine as outlined in Piper, it's REALLY hard
to see how this case should end up in the PRC.  The ONLY factor suggesting
it should go there is contract provision (along with perhaps Dong Ah's
place of incorporation, but Dong Ah's principal place of business is in the
United States).  But if the contract provision along is enough, then we've
just turned the FNC analysis back into the enforceability of the FSC.
Meanwhile, almost all of the witnesses, all of the relevant events, the
plaintiff's choice of forum, and rumors about the quality of PRC courts
(see Piper re availability of some remedy) all point to stateside
litigation.
Question 1(c)
      We might try to argue that Judge Watson's order in the First Case has
issue preclusive effect in the subsequent state court litigation on the
issue of the enforceability of the contractual forum selection clause.
Since the "rendering" court here was federal, federal common law probably
governs (Semtek), which would incorporate either AL or NY preclusion law,
depending on the 1404(a)/1406(a) transfer issue discussed above; note we
could ask the New York state court to give issue preclusive effect to Judge
Watson's stay order even if the federal court would not (Hart).  Thus, back
in Albany state court, we can move to dismiss or stay the Second Case on
the grounds that the enforceability of the forum selection clause (the
"FSC") was already decided in our favor.  We would argue that the FSC issue
is the same in state court as it is in federal court, Parker argued it
fully and lost it, and the issue was necessary to (indeed, was the sole
basis for) Judge Watson's stay order.
      There are a couple of problems.  First, Judge Watson did not issue a
final judgment.  The order might still be appealable (see above), but Judge
Watson explicitly contemplated revising the order under certain
circumstances, so the order appears to lack the requisite finality.  We can
try to argue that Judge Watson contemplates no further action on the case,
especially since we intend to comply with all of her conditions, and thus
that the order is essentially final.
      Second, it's not clear that the same law would govern a motion to
dismiss or stay in the Second Case as governed in the First Case.  It's
also not clear whether that matters, i.e., whether "issue" in "issue
preclusion" means that the rendering court had to be litigating the "issue"
under the same law as would apply in the subsequent court.  If the issue is
the enforceability of the FSC, as opposed to FNC (as seems more likely, per
above), it feels like that issue was already litigated (although, per
above, perhaps not incorporated as part of a judgment), and it shouldn't
matter which law applies.  That seems most likely.
      If, however, "issue" in "issue preclusion" does mean that the issue
had to be litigated in the rendering court under the same (or similar) law
as would apply in the subsequent court, then we have to figure out whether
the law that would Second Case is substantially the same as the law that
did to the stay order in the First Case.  If there's a conflict, no issue
preclusion; if the two legal rules are pretty similar then, perhaps it's
the same "issue."  Regarding what law did apply (or should have applied) in
the First Case, the NY Supreme Court would apply NY  procedural law and NY
choice of law principles to substantive issues; if FSC enforceability is
substantive, the NY court might ell apply AL law on FSC enforceability!

## Question 1 (24 points, two parts)

In September of 2007, Congress passed the "Federal Giveaway Bank Regulation Act of 2007" (the "FGBRA"). The FGBRA created a fund of several billion dollars, controlled by the Secretary of the Treasury, designed to provide temporary loans to banks facing liquidity trouble. Section 301 of the FGBRA provided:

It is enough to commit the tort of slander that the statement is heard; the third party who hears the statement need have no connection to either the plaintiff (who is the subject of the statement) or the defendant (who uttered it). The tort is complete when any listener hears the false statement, and a plaintiff is entitled to at least nominal damages even if the listener does not know the plaintiff. A false statement that a person committed a non-trivial crime is generally grounds for slander. Libel is the same thing as slander except that the statement is printed, not uttered orally. A newspaper reporter may be found liable for libel if she writes a story (that is subsequently printed) that is false, and does so with reckless disregard for the truth. Thus, a critical issue in a libel case against a reporter is how much the reporter knew about the truthfulness of a statement the reporter printed, including whether the reporter had any reason to doubt that truthfulness before printing.

and/or Rita in Maryland is out of the question; from the facts given, it
appears that Dean has no contacts to MD other than the fact that train on
which he was riding was in MD at the time he uttered the Harry-murderer

tenuous, but they probably are sufficient.  As noted above, the action that
gave rise to the tort was committed in MD.  True, the fact that this action
took place in MD was as gratuitous and accidental as was the tag in Grace

Restatement, MD law will thus apply to the tort.  Regarding the needs of
the interstate judicial system and the convenience aspects of the

because the witnesses and the evidence (particularly motive) are in common
and the facts are somewhat related.  In response to the efficiency
concerns, a court might be tempted to create a doctrine of SIPJ as a matter
of common law (the same way SCOTUS did for supplemental subject matter
jurisdiction ("SSMJ") in Gibbs, even absent a statute).  If so, the
doctrine would probably say that if a court can exercise IPJ over one
defendant as to one claim (claim = Liberty mutual definition), it can
exercise SIPJ over all other claims so related as that arise as part of the
same T/O.  The general argument here, again, would be efficiency, mirroring
SSMJ.

      But there are two reasons for caution here.  First, limits on SSMJ
stem from constitutional and prudential concerns about separation of powers
and court control.  Courts might feel as though they can create new legal
doctrine here with relative freedom.  IPJ, supposedly, involves a right
personal to the defendant, as well as the projection of state power into
other states.  Courts might be less willing to create new law when a
defendant's individual rights are involved, particularly in the absence of
a statute specifying the doctrine of SIPJ.  True, there was no statute
available in Gibbs, either, but statutes matter in IPJ, compare McGee to
Hanson and see also Schaeffer.  Second, one might read Finley in particular
and Owen as well as demonstrating a court wariness to read supplemental
jurisdiction (of any type) as allowing exercise of judicial power over a
PARTY who would not otherwise properly be before the court.  If so, courts
might be willing to create an SIPJ doctrine that would allow exercise of
IPJ over Dean as to Count II, since the court will already exercise IPJ
over Dean as to Count I.  But courts might refuse to create an SIPJ
doctrine that would allow exercise of IPJ over Rita on these fact, because
Rita is not otherwise before the court.  This SIPJ additional-claim-but-not-
additional-party distinction would mirror the holdings in Finley and Owen.
And for this reason it seems the least constitutionally suspect.

      Finally, note that there exist other tools, such as transfer, FNC,
and the joinder rules of FRCP to prevent undue abuse of an expanded
doctrine of SIPJ.  These tools all concern geographic concerns.  The only
problem with relying on them too heavily is that none are of constitutional
magnitude, so if Congress or a state legislature did something goofy (such
as eliminating all of these doctrines), and expanded doctrine of SIPJ might
be dangerous.  One could also engraft an element of discretion over the
exercise of SIPJ, as was true in the SSMJ context in Gibbs, although again,
if there were discretion, it would seem as though Congress could remove it.

Question 1(c)

Question 2(d)

      The only basis for a stay is Colorado River (" CR" ) (none of the
other abstention bases work, plus we didn't cover them in detail in this
class, anyway).  But CR recognizes that the federal court has an
"unflagging obligation" to exercise the jurisdiction given, with the
understanding that the fact of parallel state and federal litigation alone
is not enough.  And there's little more here to suggest a stay.  A major
factor AGAINST a stay is that Piers filed the lawsuit that he's currently
asking to be stayed.  True, he filed it in federal court, but he had to
know that CRASH!, Inc. might remove (per Hertz, the company's principle
place of business is AL, not TX, and it is incorporated in DE, so it can
remove a TX suit.)  If Piers wanted to trap CRASH!, Inc. in state court, he
should have filed first in AL state court (as he eventually did).  The two
suits have progressed about the same distance, and there can't be much of a
convenience difference when both are located in Birmingham.  Only the kind
of judicial chicanery that we saw in Clark v. Lacy could lead to a stay
here, and even there the defendant (not the plaintiff) wanted the stay.
The court should not grant the stay.

      The issue on limitations and FNC depends on choice of law.  On
limitations, York tells us we have to use state law (TX or AL, see below),
but the FNC issue poses an Erie choice as between federal FNC (as
articulated in Piper) and state (TX or AL, see below).  And there are some
serious differences in federal versus state FNC law here, so we have to
choose.

      So, Erie and FNC.  We don't know anything about the judicial system
in Mexico, so we don't know whether the Mexico system provides any remedy
at all, a precondition for FNC dismissal.  And no court would dismiss on
FNC grounds without assuring that the Mexico forum would be open, so any
court would insist on a CRASH!, Inc. waiver of limitations.  Assume that's
all taken care of, let's run through the four Erie tests we know.  FNC is
probably not outcome-determinative in the sense that the York court meant,
i.e., that the FNC ruling mandates (in a legal sense) the end of the
lawsuit, meaning the court could apply federal law.  Under the Hanna
majority, there is no federal constitutional provision, statute, or rule on
point, so we look to the twin aims of Erie.  Here, the difference in FNC
law might well induce a choice of state court over federal court at the
time of filing, leading to forum shopping (although it's hard to see much
of an argument for inequitable administration of laws), suggesting state
law.  Looking to primary conduct (Harlan, Hanna concurrence), it's hard to
see how the conduct of a stateside corporation would change based on the
availability or lack of availability of FNC law with respect to foreign
consumers.  The best argument one can make here is that an extremely
liberal application of FNC law to a corporation that sells most of its
products in a city near the Mexico border might induce the corporation to
adopt a more aggressive business style, since it might know that it will
defend more suits in Mexico, where liability is presumably less likely and
less costly.  One might also argue that the corporation might place a forum
selection clause in its sales contracts.  The first seems a bit of a
stretch, and the second really isn't the sort of primary conduct Harlan was
worried about, so Harlan's concurrence suggests federal law.  In terms of
the accommodation-of-interests approach, one might argue that FNC is not a
particularly strong federal policy; it's judge-made law of venue.  The
federal interest is merely convenience; the state interest (FNC not
available in AL, rarely available in TX) might be in ease of administration
in the sense that the FNC issue will be rarely litigated in TX (never in
AL), so no line-drawing problems, and in preserving the plaintiff's choice
of forum.  It is, concededly, hard to understand how one can accommodate
both the state and the federal interests can be accommodated here, but this
seems like a case in which Justice Ginsburg might say that the federal
interest should simply yield to the state policy choice.

      If federal law, is this a good candidate for FNC dismissal?  A close
call.  We do have a foreign plaintiff, as we did in Piper.  And Mexico law
might govern the tort action (TX = last event necessary, which is the
crash, suggesting Mexico law; less clear under AL 2d Restatement approach,
one might argue that the center of gravity in this case is TX because
that's where the purchase was made). These facts are similar to Piper, and
they point to Mexico.  But the purchase was made in TX, and the evidence
regarding the tire rubber is in AL (apparently).  The plaintiff was the
purchaser (who took the product to Mexico), not someone removed from the
stateside transaction (as was true of the plaintiffs in Piper).  Perhaps
most importantly, the defendant knew that the plaintiff wanted to use the
bike in Mexico and still made the sale with oral and written promises.  My
recommendation is no FNC dismissal.

      If state law, which state?  Under Van Dusen and Ferens, the issue is
whether the Texas state (and thus the Texas federal) court had IPJ over the
def (venue not being an issue in a removed case).  Clearly so.  The def
really does do business in Texas, including the sale in this case.  So this
transfer was under 1404(a), and it came from a court with IPJ and venue.
That means TX choice of law rules apply.  FNC is likely to be characterized
as "procedural" for horizontal choice of law purposes, in which case Texas
would apply its own law.  It would seem really odd for TX to apply Mexican
FNC law to decide whether to dismiss the case in favor of a Mexican suit.
If FNC is characterized as substantive, Mexico FNC law would apply, and we
don't know the content of Mexican FNC law.  Under TX FNC law, given the
location of the CRASH!, Inc. store, neither a TX or an AL forum would be
oppressive, so no FNC dismissal.

      But what about limitations?  Under the reasoning above, TX choice of
law rules apply; we don't know with absolute certainty whether TX considers
limitations periods to be substantive or procedural for choice of law
principles, but we do know that TX follows the First Restatement, and under
it, most of the time SoLs are procedural.  If so, as seems likely, the
court should dismiss under TX's 2-year period.    If substantive, then
Mexican law will apply, and we would not dismiss under Mexico's 3-year
period.

Question 2(e)

      We're looking at preclusion; to begin, the pendency of the appeal
does not affect finality for either issue or claim preclusion.

      The theory on getting the AL state court to dismiss in favor of an
action is Mexico is that the issue of where this suit should be litigated
was decided in the federal court action.  But given AL law absolutely
prohibiting an FNC dismissal, it's hard to see how this issue arises in the
AL state court litigation.  There's no FNC issue to arise in the state
court action because whatever else is true, federal FNC law does not apply
in state court.  We might also have a problem with the necessary-to-the-
decision element of issue preclusion (we have mutuality, so we don't need
to worry about that), given the rulings in-the-alternative.  But the lack
of a  similar issue here is probably dispositve.

      The limitations dismissal is harder.  We need to know whether the
limitations dismissal is on the merits (we have same parties and same
transaction and a final judgment) under somebody's law.  We have to decide
whose law, because the law of the state in which the dismissing federal
court sat (AL) does not consider the dismissal on the merits for claim
preclusion purposes.    Probably Texas again.  The transfer under 1404(a)
(see above) is supposed to be a change of courtroom only.  Semtek didn't
cover this situation because there was no transfer.  If it's just a change
of courtroom with no effect on the law applied, the state in which the
dismissing court "sits" is TX, not AL, and so the dismissal has claim
preclusive effect.



Question 2(f)

      Numerosity is probably met; true, we have only 30 plaintiffs, but
they're spread out geographically, making it harder to sue.  Commonality
will be subsumed within predominance, so see below.  Typicality and
representativeness are probably met; Piers' claim depends on bad rubber,
and if there are other bikes that had bad rubber, this claim would be the
same as those of the other folks.  The problems are predominance and
superiority.  CRASH!, Inc.'s defense is likely to be that terrain in each
of the accidents was not "reasonable."  That requires an assessment of the
terrain at each site, which is certainly not common.  Part of this might
depend on the substantive law of the jurisdiction, meaning that it might be
hard to know whether the reasonableness of the terrain is in fact a defense
to a strict liability claim under these circumstances.  That, again, may
depend on the law of each state (even under Restatement 2 the law of the
state in which the accident happened would be a likely choice for this kind
of issue).  These concerns mitigate against predominance and superiority.
The amount of the claims involved cuts both ways.  $5K is a small claims
court case.  $150K is enough to get a reasonable attorney.  If there are
more of the latter, then it's not clear that the class action is really
needed.

Notes on Question 1:  Policy question, lots of different directions in
which students could go.

Rule 126 goes beyond anything required by Bell Atlantic and Iqbal in that
it requires not just that detailed factual allegations be made but that
actual evidence be attached to the complaint.  This rule is wholly
inconsistent with the idea of notice pleading, and goes well beyond code
pleading.  It essentially requires a plaintiff to prove a prima facie case
in order to survive a motion to dismiss.

Bonus points if students realize that this rule is not really a rule of
pleading but a form of advanced initial disclosure that must be made even
if the defendant does not appear in the lawsuit.  This is not required,
however, as we did not study initial disclosure.  To get full credit, an
answer must refer to the fact that the rule imposes a form of discovery on
the plaintiff at the pleading stage, and ask whether this is appropriate at
the pleading stage.  References to the requirement of a document production
will receive full credit.



There is virtually no argument that a right to counsel applies in this type
of lawsuit.  Even under Lassiter, the case for mandatory counsel would
stink, and it definitely stinks under Turner.  There is no apparent danger
of incarceration at all, and thus under Lassiter no presumptive case for
counsel.  But there is an argument that Rule 126 might be constitutionally
required under Turner (not a strong argument, but an argument).  One can
recite the Matthews factors formulaically, but the basic idea here is that
the Delaware Supreme Court has attempted to find a way short of counsel to
provide access to justice for credit card debt collection defendants.
Thus, Rule 126 is analogous to the "form" to which SCOTUS attributed nearly
mythical properties in Turner.  At present, however, without a recognized
constitutional right of access to justice, this argument is fairly weak.

One argument here, again invoking the Matthews factors (the third one this
time), is that it is hard to see how much of a harm collection agencies
suffer here, unless they are depending on default judgments, which (as the
Delaware Supreme Court stated) are disfavored by public policy.  The first
thing any competent lawyer would do in this kind of lawsuit is to issue a
request for production of documents that focuses precisely on the documents
required by Rule 126.

On the other hand, rules of procedure specific to a particular type of
lawsuit are always messy.  Line-drawing problems are always present.  Would
Rule 126 apply if, for example, a debt collection lawsuit is filed for a
debt that includes both credit card and other kinds of debt? Contracts
suits in which we'd expect the defendant to have greater access to the
contact documents (as in employment and benefits contracts)?  What about
oral contracts?  Lawsuits not based on contracts that do not involve
documents?

The rule could affect the market for credit card debt.  If full
documentation is required on each and every debt, the market for debt might
become less liquid.  But perhaps not by all that much.  Again, these would
be the first documents a debtor's lawyer would request.  Thus, the effect
on liquidity might depend on how often debtors are forced to self-represent
in these cases.
Question 2(a):

There are six potential options for locations to file lawsuits:  Delaware
state and federal courts, Massachusetts state and federal courts, and New
Hampshire state and federal courts.

Rule 126 will definitely apply in DE state court.  That's bad, because we
apparently don't have the required documents.  Would it apply in either MA
or NH state courts?  No.  The issue of whether a plaintiff in a credit card
debt collection lawsuit must attach certain specified documents to its
complaint is procedural for horizontal choice of law purposes (since the
scope of "procedural" in horizontal choice of law is broad, and almost
anything that is arguably procedural is considered so).  So MA and NH state
courts would use their own law on whether we would have to attach the
documents to a complaint against Pansy and/or Draco, and they don't have
anything close to Rule 126.

Would Rule 126 apply in any of the three federal courts?  Because NH and MA
state courts would not apply Rule 126, neither would NH or MA federal
courts.  Even if the issue of documents needed for the complaint were
considered "substantive" for vertical choice of law, a NH (MA) federal
court would use NH (MA) choice of law rules, Klaxon, and as described
above, a NH (MA) state court would use its own law under a horizontal
procedural/substantive distinction, so no Rule 126.

What about a Delaware federal court?  Erie, four tests.  Under York, Rule
126 would determine the outcome of the case if we sue without attaching
documents, so Rule 126 would apply.  Under the Hannah majority, Fed. Rs.
Civ. P. 8(a)(2), 12(b)(6) require that a complaint include only a short,
plain statement showing that the plaintiff is entitled to relief, not
documents/evidence.[1]  And it is hard to see how these rules expand,
modify, or alter substantive rights, so they would appear to be valid.
Thus, under Hannah majority step one, it would appear that Rule 126 would
not apply.  Could we do a Walker on these Rules, by narrowly construing
them?  Perhaps.  This would actually look a lot like an application of
Justice Ginsburg's opinion in Shady Grove.  Perhaps the idea here would be
to say that Fed. Rs. Civ. P. 8(a)(2), 12(b)(6) speak to the issue of what
must be alleged in the complaint, not what must be attached to it.[2]
Under this theory, we would say that there is no federal law on the
subject, no contradiction, and state law can apply.  We might reach the
same answer if we try to balance the state and federal interests involved.
The federal interest is in maintaining a transsubstantive pleading rules
(the Twiqbal standard, although that doesn't matter).  Delaware's interests
are specific to this type of litigation and well grounded in an
investigation of what is actually happening in its courts, as disclosed by
the detailed comments accompanying the rule.  There is at least an argument
that DE's interests in this specific type of lawsuit, interests based in a
serious investigation, might trump the federal interests here.

      Under Harlan's Hannah concurrence, the issue is difficult.  On the
one hand, it would be a stretch to argue that Rule 126 would affect how
consumers use credit cards and whether they become delinquent.  But the
Rule might affect the collection market for credit card debt; purchasers of
debt would definitely need to make sure they procured documents underlying
each and every debt purchased.  That could get cumbersome.  See answer to
Question 1.  The question here might turn on what kind of primary conduct
Harlan was concerned about; the initial credit card user's primary conduct
(and that of the credit card issuer), or the collection market for credit
card debt.  I'd go with the former, so I'd say no effect in primary
conduct, and given Fed. Rs. Civ. P. 8(a)(2), 12(b)(6), Rule 126 does NOT
apply.

      To summarize:  Rule 126 would apply in DE state court, might apply in
a DE federal court, and won't apply anywhere else.

      Next question:  could we sue both Pansy and Draco in a single lawsuit
anywhere?  We could probably do so in DE state court.  Under Rule 20
(recall same rules of civ pro everywhere), there are common questions
everywhere, such as whether Rule 126 applies, whether the chain of
transfers from the original credit card issuer to G-Credit was valid, etc.
The harder issue is whether Pansy & Draco's lawsuit comes from the same
T/O.  Per below, it would appear as though the two debts are separate T/O
for preclusion purposes, because by analogy to coupons and bonds, we want
it to be possible to sell the debts separately on a collection market (see
Cromwell v. County of Sac).  But the policies underlying Rule 20 are
different; here, the two debts have a great deal in common, so we'd want
them to be litigated together.  So we're OK under the rules.  What about
personal jurisdiction?  Probably OK here as well, on either a general
jurisdiction theory (if Pansy and Draco are still domiciled in Delaware,
see below) or a specific jurisdiction theory (the original credit card
debts arose from charges they made in Delaware, so plenty of International
Shoe minimum contacts, with the long-arm no problem because it goes to the
limits of the constitution).

      Delaware federal court:  The previous paragraph suggests that we'd be
OK under FRCP 20 to sue Pansy and Draco jointly, and that under FRCP
4(k)(1)(A), the Delaware federal court could piggyback onto the Delaware
state court's IPJ so as to exercise IPJ over both Pansy and Draco.  Could
we lay venue?  Yes; again the debts arose in Delaware, and that's "a
substantial part of the events . . . giving rise to the claim occurred"
under section 1291(a)(2).

      What about subject matter jurisdiction?  There are two issues:
First, where are Pansy and Draco domiciled, second, can any federal court
exercise supplemental jurisdiction over a suit by us against Draco, given
that it is AIC-deficient on its own?  On the first issue, the problem is
that we are domiciled in NH and MA, so if Draco (Pansy) is domiciled in MA
(NH), we lack diversity.  Pansy and Draco were obviously domiciled
initially in DE, and if they are still domiciled there, we have complete
diversity.  Did their domiciles shift, Draco's to MA and Pansy's to NH,
when they lost their jobs, their house, and had to move in with their
parents?  Not 100% clear.  Certainly their permanent ties to DE were
severed, but they continue to have something of a marriage (communicating
somewhat regularly and applying for many of the same jobs, suggesting a
desire to live in the same location).  Perhaps more importantly, Draco
(Pansy) does not appear to have laid down serious roots in MA (NH).  Draco
(Pansy) has been in MA (NH) for a couple of years now, but time is not
dispositive (students attending 4-year colleges typically don't change
their domiciles).  Job applications appear to be focusing as much on DE as
anywhere else.  It's hard to know whether Draco's (Pansy's) housing
situation with his (her) parents is permanent.  Overall this is a close
call.

      Now, suppose that Pansy and Draco are domiciled in DE, so we have
complete diversity, but that leaves the Exxon/AIC/supplemental jurisdiction
issue.  There is no arising under jurisdiction in the picture.  Our lawsuit
against Pansy has $90K in controversy, so it's the anchoring claim.  The
question is whether we can tack on Draco's $40K lawsuit, which is AIC
deficient.  The arguments are familiar from Exxon.  On the one hand, under
1367(b), Draco's lawsuit is a claim by a plaintiff (us) against a person
(Draco) made a party under Rule 20, and the exercise of jurisdiction over
Draco's lawsuit would arguably "would be inconsistent with the
jurisdictional requirements of section 1332" since Draco's lawsuit is AIC
deficient.  On the other hand, in Exxon, SCOTUS appeared to conclude that
"the jurisdictional requirements of section 1332" refer only to complete
diversity, not the AIC, in which case we can proceed against both Pansy and
Draco simultaneously in DE federal court.

      What about MA (NH) state courts?  See above on the Rule 20 issues.
One problem whether either court can exercise personal jurisdiction over
either defendant.  General jurisdiction theories for (MA for Draco, NH for
Pansy) depend on the domicile issue, see above.  We could pretty easily tag
Draco (Pansy) in MA (NH), so that would be a big factor in our favor.  The
far bigger issue is whether we could sue both Draco AND Pansy together in a
single lawsuit in either a MA or NH state court.  The problem is that Draco
appears to have absolutely no contacts to NH other than the fact that he
occasionally visits Pansy, and vice versa for Pansy.  There is the
additional factor for Pansy that has applied for jobs in MA, but none of
these jobs has come through yet.  None of Draco's (Pansy's) contacts to NH
(MA) related to the subject matter of the lawsuit.  Absent a tag of both in
either NH or MA, it's hard to see how a MA (NH) court could exercise IPJ
over Pansy (Draco).  Of course, as neither Pansy nor Draco appears to have
counsel or be able to afford counsel, we could sue them and see if they
fail to raise IPJ (seems likely), in which case they would have waived the
issue.

      What about MA (NH) federal court?  The SMJ issues are the same as
those in DE federal court.  Under Rule 4(k)(1)(A), the personal
jurisdiction issues are the same as those in MA and NH state courts.  The
problem is venue.  Because we can lay venue in DE federal court under
1391(a)(2), that means 1391(a)(3) does not apply, and there does not appear
to be a basis to lay venue against both Pansy and Draco simultaneously in

Assume the following, all of which may be (and probably is) entirely fictional.

Under federal common law, judgments against a plaintiff based on a failure of the plaintiff to file a lawsuit within the applicable limitations period are not entitled to claim preclusion effect. Under the laws of New Hampshire and Massachusetts, judgments based on a failure of a plaintiff to file within the applicable limitations period are entitled to claim preclusion effect.

In 2005, Congress passed the "Professional Malpractice Limitations Act," or the PMLA. The PMLA provided that in any action in federal court in the nature of lawyer, medical, engineering, or other "professional" malpractice (meaning negligence by one of these professionals) or by an organization providing professional (medical, legal, engineering) services, the applicable limitations period is one year.

In New Hampshire and Massachusetts, the applicable limitations period for actions in the nature of professional (medical, legal, engineering services) malpractice is six years.

An issue that sometimes arises in the context of limitations periods is when the limitations clock begins to "accrue" or tick. In other words, suppose that a statute of limitations for a certain kind of tort is X years. At what point in time does the X-year period begin to run? Broadly speaking, there are two general approaches to this question: the "misconduct rule" and the "discovery rule." If the "misconduct" rule applies, the applicable statute of limitations begins to run on the date that the act or omission giving rise to the claim occurs, assuming of course that some harm eventually results (or else there would be no tort). In other words, under the "misconduct rule," the limitations period begins to run on the date that the defendant commits the relevant misconduct (the act or omission); the clock begins to run when the act or omission occurs, not when the injury/harm occurs. If the "discovery rule" applies, in contrast, the limitations period begins to run instead on the date that the claimant first knows about, or first should have known about, the act or omission giving rise to the claim. In other words, under the "discovery rule," the limitations period begins to run on the date that the plaintiff first discovers, or should have discovered, that the defendant committed some kind of misconduct. This might be when some hidden injury or disease manifests itself in an unmistakable way.

To illustrate the difference between the "misconduct rule" and the "discovery rule": Suppose a patient visits the doctor on January 2, 1996. The doctor tells the patient that she is healthy. In fact, a non-negligent doctor would have diagnosed the patient with a serious illness. The patient does not find out that she in fact has been suffering all along from a serious illness until she visits a second doctor on May 4, 1999. Under the "misconduct rule," the applicable limitations period began to run on January 2, 1996. Under the "discovery rule," the applicable limitations period might not begin to run until May 4, 1999.

Federal courts have long used the discovery rule. New Hampshire and Massachusetts use the "misconduct" rule.

## Selected Provisions of the National Medical Malpractice Reform Act (the "NMMRA")

*Reminder: this is the federal statute, the one Congress passed.*

> *Section 601: **Findings:** The Congress finds that the process of detecting cancerous masses suffers from the lack of a national depository of radiological images that would allow radiologists to compare images for a particular patient to images in the depository that are known to be cancerous or non-cancerous. The lack of a depository causes thousands of mistaken or missed diagnoses of cancer each year, costing the Medicare and Medicaid programs millions of dollars and having a deleterious effect upon interstate commerce.*

> *Section 602: **Establishment of a National Registry:** The Secretary of Health and Human Services is hereby directed to establish a National Registry of radiological images. The Secretary shall establish a classification system of such images, indexing them in a manner that he shall determine, but such indexing shall include classification by area of the body imaged, whether cancer was ultimately found, the type of cancer (if any), as well as the socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of the patient (age, race, gender, height, weight, and medical history).*

> *Section 603: **Deposit of Electronic Images:** Any provider of medical services who receives any funds under the Medicare or Medicaid programs, and who produces, receives, examines, or obtains any radiological image of any patient, shall forward an electronic copy of that image to the National Registry, along with all information about the patient required to enable the Secretary to index the image pursuant to Section 602 of this statute. The information forwarded to the Secretary about each patient shall be updated in a timely manner to include the diagnosis ultimately made for each patient.*

> *Section 604: **Secretarial Power To Implement:** The Secretary shall have the power promulgate rules to implement Sections 601-603 of this statute, including rules that define any terms in these Sections.*

> *Section 901: **Bloc Grant Program:** The Congress finds that the system of adjudicating medical malpractice suits in courts has caused providers of medical services and their insurers to suffer from crushing attorneys' fees, insurance premiums, and other costs.*

> *Accordingly, the Secretary of Health and Human Services shall establish and administer a system of bloc grants, which shall be funded by yearly appropriations this Congress shall make. > *Section 902: **Priority in the Bloc Grant Program:** In allocating funding, the Secretary shall give priority to states whose administrative schemes include any or all of the following:*

Question 1(a)

      Regarding the MTD, do we have venue, IPJ over Pince, and SMJ?  We
laid venue properly because a substantial portion of the events occurred in

seems possible but unlikely.  If the court buys it, it will look to the
other Smith/Grable factors to assess whether exercising federal
jurisdiction any time a plaintiff would prove a state-law cause of action
by citing a def's failure to comply with a federal law of questionable
constitutionality would sweep lots of cases (or too few) into the federal
system (seems like not too many), and whether this issue is primarily legal
or factual (on violation of the standard of care it seems like both, an
application of undisputed facts to unclear law, but medmal causation is
always intensely factual and messy).  This feels like no Smith/Grable.

      There is no hope for Colorado River abstention because there at
present no parallel proceeding, only a threat of one, and we did not study
the other abstention bases.

      Did Congress want this kind of case in federal court?  On the one

doctrines, and supplemental SMJ, for guidance.  Almost medmal law is state
tort law; there are few federal issues.  But the NMMRA does provide some
federal law, obviously, such as the national registry at issue in Pince's
case.  The administrative scheme Congress's duly empowered agent (the
Secretary) suggested for the states, as set up in the MSMMRA, is complex,
and federal declaratory judgment or garden variety DJ actions could
interfere with it.  Medmal and regulating (licensing) the medical
profession is traditional state business, but it is becoming increasingly
federalized (like so much else).  There are no tax or criminal law issues.
 And then there are the factors above discussed in Smith/Grable; more

Question 3

      There are three broad classes of issues:  IPJ over SM; the lien order
(a procedural due process =  PDP issue); the PDP status of the whole NH
Agency ("NHA") administrative scheme.

      IPJ over SM:  First, we have to see whether there is IPJ over SM,
even though the proceeding is before an administrative agency.  Otherwise,
a state could evade IPJ limits entirely, and thus project its coercive
power anywhere around the globe, simply by creating administrative agencies
to adjudicate disputes.   So we analyze the IPJ issues as though the NHA
were a court.   SM does not have overwhelming contacts to NH for general
IPJ, so specific IPJ:  minimum contacts, International Shoe.  Here, SM
solicited, but did not actually provide, business/services in NH.  And it
used newspapers of limited circulation, not a broad medium such as the
Internet, to do so.  This seems like purposeful availment.  Further, NH
(through the MSMMRA, section 302(b)) has a long-arm specific to this kind
of solicitation, putting medical providers (like SM) on notice, see
Shaeffer & McGee, as opposed to Hanson.  Against all this, we weight the
fact that SM does not provide services or ship products into NH.  I'd say
the exercise of IPJ is OK here, but if not, SM should file an appropriate
motion raising this issue (even if there is no explicit rule in the NHA
allowing for such a motion.  The NHA can't prevent SM from raising a
constitutional defense by refusing to provide a procedural mechanism for
raising it.

      The lien order:  One way to analyze is to follow Schnaidach and
simply assess the PDP status of the scheme leading to the lien order.  If
we do that:  The order was imposed without notice to SM or an opportunity
to allow SM to dispute the merits and on a blisteringly fast time schedule
(within hours of filing of the complaint).  Pince need not post a bond to
protect SM against consequences of erroneous imposition, and indeed, it is
not clear that SM could even recover for such consequences at all.  Yes,
Pince had to produce sworn affidavits from competent witnesses (not just
allegations from lawyers), but there are two problems with this
requirement:  First, the Deciding Officer ("DO") does not adjudicate
whether the affidavits provide sufficient factual support for the
conclusion that malpractice occurred (i.e., their persuasiveness), only
that the affiant had the right kind of credentials and concluded that
malpractice occurred.  Second, there is no requirement of any evidentiary
support on the amount of the lien; and here, it appears that the amount may
have been abusive, with a lot of punitive damages in the mix.  But the
scheme does have elements that can save it.  Most important of these is
that it is REALLY easy to get this lien order dissolved; all that SM need
to is file a copy of an insurance policy with a sufficiently high liability
limit, something SM surely has.  In effect, then, this whole scheme has the
same legal consequence as a NH law requiring an entity like SM to maintain
adequate insurance, which is pretty innocuous.  Second, the adjudication on
the merits will occur VERY fast, which will resolve the lien order anyway.
A close call so far.  For me, the key point is that there is no reason for
this scheme.  As in Doehr, the defendant (here, SM) isn't going anywhere,
and there is no evidence that it will hide its assets.

      Another way to analyze the lien order  is to start with Matthews test
(Doehr says apply this to old property):  What is the procedural
improvement SM would say should have been adopted?   Probably a pre-
imposition hearing with opportunity to submit evidence.  Pince's interest
in the lien order?  Low; see previous discussion re fact that SM is not
about to hide assets for flee.  SM's interest?  Medium.  The lien may put
it in technical default on its mortgage and other legal obligations, but it
is really easy to get the lien order dissolved.  Risk of erroneous
deprivation?  Very high, particularly as to the amount (see above); no
adversarial process in place, SM doesn't get to submit evidence.  Added
value of the additional procedure?  High; allowing both parties to submit
evidence is a classic way to get good information on the merits.  Expense
of the additional process?  Not all that high; the procedure on the merits
is highly accelerated anyway, so a request for a lien could simply be
folded into adjudication on the merits.

      The overall scheme:  We can get some guidance from the PDP "mini-
codes" in Vitek and in the Restatement of Judgments (2d) Sec 83.  Neither
authority is directly binding, but both can help us figure out what should
be required from a new adjudicatory scheme.  Both begin by requiring
adequate notice, which we have (service as per FRCP 4), see SMMRSA 504.
Vitek requires a "hearing . . . in person" and an opportunity to present
witnesses, whereas Sec 83 requires only an opportunity to submit evidence
and legal argument.  The SMMRSA provides the latter, not the former, but my
sense is that's probably enough; adjudication on the papers is now the norm
in federal courts (via MTD or SumJudg motions), and there is no requirement
of oral argument in that context.  Sec 83 requires a formulation of issues
by reference to specific rules; Vitek does not require this; again, I think
Sec 83 has this right, and the SMMRSA probably provides enough here.  Each
party can submit evidence by affidavit and argument by written brief, and
medmal law is highly developed.  Sec 83 requires a rule of finality, and we
have that.  Vitek requires an independent decisionmaker, and we have that.
 Nothing requires that the decisionmaker be a lawyer (see Elliot).  Vitek
requires a written statement of reasons by the deciionmaker, Sec 83 does
not.  The SMMRSA does not provide that.  From our study of FRCP 52, we know
that such statements are helpful because they induce higher quality
decision making, allowing better appellate review, and help us figure out
preclusion.  But there is no appellate review here (SMMRSA 517), and the
other two policies are probably not of constitutional importance, despite
Vitek.  Vitek requires effective and timely notice of the foregoing rights,
and we have that.  Note that to the extent that we are providing less
protection than was required in Vitek:  there was a big liberty interest at
stake there (stigma), while here, it's just money.  Forget about counsel at
state expense; this is garden variety tort stuff, with no loss of physical
liberty in the picture.

      But there is a lot here that makes me nervous in a PDP sense.  The 7th
A's right to a jury trial has never been applied to the states, so there's
no constitutional issue, but still, there is a long tradition of jury
adjudication of this kind of issue, particularly punitive damages, which
often involve a sense of the community (juries are better on that) as to
how much the def should be punished.  The public rights exception would not
apply, were there a jury right at issue, because no sovereign is a party
acting as a regulator.  There is no appeal.  There is no serious discovery
(just of the medical file).  The pace of the adjudication is blistering,
even though medmal issues are complex.  There is no live hearing, even if
one is not constitutionally required.  Per above, there is no written
explanation of the result.  The amounts involved can be high.  All this
adds up to making me very nervous in a PDP sense, even if I cannot point to
a particular aspect of the scheme that violates the PDP principles.




      Question 4(a)

      There is no information on numerosity, but there are probably enough
folks who had consulted SM for radiological matters.  There is probably
commonality on the issue of whether SM acts illegally by refusing to
participate in the national registry.  Pince has the same claim as the
class (she says she was hurt by the failure to comply with the national
registry), so she's probably typical, and she seems as adequate a
representative as any.  There are no inherent structural conflicts in the
class to the extent that the whole class seeks forward-looking, injunction
relief.  There may be some danger that Pince would sell the class down the
river for a larger damages award on her malpractice claim, but that could
only happen in a settlement, and FRCP 23(e) should take care of that.  The
class seeks an injunction, so seems like a good 23(b)(2) class.

      Question 4(b)

      Erie:  federal discovery rule versus state misconduct rule.  Apply
four Erie test.  Under Harlan's test, when the limitations period starts is
tied strongly how long a plaintiff may wait between the events giving rise
to the suit and filing; whether this affects primary conduct is exactly the
same question as whether the limitations period itself does.  The argument
re affecting primary conduct is stronger for large entities that have a lot
of litigation, such as insurance companies; my sense is that a medical
provider, even a large one, doesn't fit, so I'd say no affect, apply
federal law, do not dismiss lawsuit.  For Hanna majority, the key issue is
whether the discovery rule is considered part of the federal statute
specifying the limitations period or a common law rule.  If the former,
apply it in step one (clearly applicable, probably valid under Congress'
power to regulate federal courts), and don't dismiss lawsuit.  If the
latter, go to step two, twin aims, predictably different results leads to
forum shopping, apply state law, and dismiss.  But this makes no sense
because in state court the suit would not be dismissed because the longer
state limitations period would apply.  Same thing with York:  it's outcome
determinative only if we narrowly look at the discovery rule versus the
misconduct rule; but that's silly because we're not looking at what would
happen in state court (again, the case would survey due to the longer
limitations period).  For Ginsburg, we look to the interests and attempt to
preserve federal and state polices, but nothing makes any sense except the
idea that the same law should govern BOTH the length of the limitations
period and when the clock starts ticking.

      All this points to a flaw in this whole analysis:  once we've decided
that federal law determines the length of the limitations period, it would
not make sense to apply state law as to when that period starts.  The two
decisions are tied too closely together.  A legislature would consider when
the clock starts ticking together with how long it should run.  That's what
happened here.  The state starts the clock ticking immediately but lets it
run longer.  The feds don't start the clock ticking immediately, but when
it does start ticking, it goes for a shorter time.  This is analogous to a
criticism of the Second Restatement's issue-by-issue approach in the HCOL
context; pull issues apart and nothing may make sense.  The federal
discovery rule should apply, and the suit should live.

      Question 4(c)(i)

      No.  It's not a final order because the remaining claims are still
pending.  There are no FRCP 54(b) express findings, and no double
discretion certification, no injunction, no extraordinary circumstances or
irreparable harm for mandamus, and no reason why this can't be appealed
later for Cohen.

      Question 4(c)(ii)

      The pendency of an appeal does not affect the finality (for
preclusion purposes) of the MA federal court judgment, so it's irrelevant.

      Issue preclusion:  No.  The only possible common issue is whether
Pince can recover damages.  But the federal court decided the damages issue
on the grounds of a limitations period that the NHA will not apply (because
it will apply the longer NH period under a HCOL limitations-usually-
procedural analysis), so there is no "same issue."  Limitations was
actually litigated and necessary to the decision, and we have same parties,
but that doesn't matter without the same issue.

      Claim preclusion:  We have a final judgment, and same party we are
trying to preclude (Pince).  But it's not clear as to whether the
limitations dismissal will be considered on the merits in the intersystem
preclusion setting.  This probably turns on whether the MA federal court
was sitting in DJ or FQJ, see question 1(a) response.  If the former, per
Semtek, MA law (state where the MA federal court sits), we are told to
assume MA law says a limitations dismissal is on the merits, and the NHA
proceeding dies.  If FQJ, then federal law, we are told federal law says a
limitations dismissal is not on the merits, NHA proceeding goes on.

      Question 4(d)

      Whether the agency ruling can have any preclusive effect may depend on
the PDP issues discussed above, per Restatement section 83.  Assume those
are OK and we can just apply preclusion law.

      Issue preclusion:  No.  Pince won in the NHA!  SM can't preclude Pince
on an issue when Pince won on it.  SM can't even say that the $2M award
means no punitive damages, because we don't know what the DO actually
decided; the DO might have awarded 1.5M in compensatory and $1.5M in

      Claim preclusion:  probably , but only as to the federal court damages
claim, not the injunction or the class action.  By analogy to the rule in
Marresse, the NHA had no jurisdiction to certify a class or award an
injunction, so its ruling cannot preclude an effort to seek those remedies.
 Re claim preclusion on the federal court damages claim:  we have the exact
same claim and a final decision, and it is unquestionably on the merits.
Pince can't recover more money in a different court on the exact same
lawsuit.

## Background Law

Oregon Statutes Section 5541 provides as follows:

> *In any action sounding in tort and involving a plea for damages for emotional distress, a successful plaintiff shall be entitled to damages according to the following schedule:*
>
> *(a) emotional harm caused by the death of the plaintiff or of a member of the plaintiff's immediate family: $150,000.*
>
> *(b) emotional harm caused by severe bodily injury to the plaintiff or a member of the plaintiff's immediate family: $130,000*
>
> *(c) emotional harm not caused by bodily harm to any person: $80,000.*

Under federal common law, there is no limit to the amount an individual may recover for emotional distress; the issue is submitted to the jury, which may award an amount it deems proper (subject to ordinary supervision by the trial judge).

Arizona's common law regarding damages for emotional distress is identical to federal common law.

In 2002, the California Legislature passed the "Emotional Distress Lawsuits Are Out Of Control Act of 2002," known the "EDLAOOCA." In relevant part, the EDLAOOCA provides as follows.

> *1) Findings: The California Legislature notes a proliferation of lawsuits against California companies in California courts (and in courts outside of California) in which individuals sought damages for emotional distress. The proliferation of these lawsuits has caused a detrimental effect upon the California economy. Companies employing citizens of California have begun to move their operations elsewhere to avoid the reach of the law, causing job losses to other states. Companies that have remained in California have begun to engage in excessively cautious behavior to forestall lawsuits claiming emotional harm.*
>
> *2) The laws of the State of California providing a right to sue for emotional harm or distress are hereby amended so as to make them subject to the following limits:*
>
> > *a) In any action sounding in tort and including a plea for damages for emotional distress, plaintiffs may proceed only as individuals. There shall be no joinder by one plaintiff in a lawsuit with another plaintiff, and no aggregation devices shall be maintained.*
> >
> > *b) In any action sounding in tort, involving a plea for damages for emotional distress, and not involving the death of the plaintiff or any member of the plaintiff's family, a plaintiff who proves that she has suffered emotional*

<!-- page: 4 -->

> > *harm shall be entitled to an award of exactly $20,000, no more and no less.*

The law described above applies in full to the common law tort of "Outrage" or "Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress." California and Oregon both recognize this tort, but the two states define the tort slightly differently.

- Under the common law of California, "Outrage" is defined as follows: "One who by extreme or outrageous conduct intentionally *or recklessly* causes severe emotional distress to another is subject to liability for such emotional distress" (emphasis added). The California common law of tort defines "recklessness" as the "failure to exercise due care to eliminate a known risk of harm."

- Under the common law of Oregon, in contrast, "Outrage" is defined as follows: "One who by extreme or outrageous conduct intentionally causes severe emotional distress to another is subject to liability for such emotional distress."

- Thus, under the law of Oregon, a plaintiff must prove that the defendant *intentionally* committed the extreme or outrageous conduct, whereas in California a plaintiff may succeed by proving that a defendant's acts were *reckless* and need not (but may) prove intent.

- Under the law of both Oregon and California, the tort of "Outrage" is not complete until the plaintiff suffers the emotional distress that gives rise to the claim.

Arizona law of Outrage is identical to that of California.

California follows the Second Restatement on Choice of Law. Oregon and Arizona follow the First Restatement on choice of law.

All states in this exam have long-arm statutes authorizing exercise of jurisdiction to the limits of the U.S. Constitution.

California, Arizona, and Oregon all have state civil procedure provisions identical to Fed. R. Civ. P. 23, and they follow federal law interpretations of Rule 23 with respect to their respective counterpart provisions.

The law of all relevant states, as well as federal common law, includes the doctrine of respondeat superior. Oregon's issue preclusion doctrine requires full mutuality. California's preclusion law is the same as federal common law. Arizona has completely abandoned the mutuality requirement.

By way of explanation: Fed. Rs. Evid. 702–703, 705 govern the admissibility of "expert testimony." Expert testimony is evidence provided by an "expert witness." An expert witness is someone who, by virtue of specialized training, knowledge, skill, or experience, is able to assist a trier of fact in understanding part of a case that would ordinarily be beyond the trier of fact's ability to adjudicate. A common use of expert testimony is in medical malpractice cases. Medical malpractice is a tort closely related to a standard negligence claim. In a medical malpractice case, a plaintiff alleges that a doctor provided negligent medical care (meaning care below the applicable standard of care), and that she was injured as a result. In such a case, then, a plaintiff might call as a witness a physician from the same specialty as the defendant's to testify that the medical care the defendant provided was negligent and that the negligence proximately caused the plaintiff's injuries. For example, a plaintiff who alleges that her former radiologist failed to diagnose cancer from MRI images might call another radiologist as an expert witness. This expert witness might testify that a radiologist of ordinary skill and exercising ordinary care would have diagnosed cancer from her MRI images.

Expert witnesses pose difficult problems for judges and juries. By definition, their testimony concerns matters that a trier of fact cannot easily evaluate. As a result, the

<!-- page: 5 -->

provisions of Rule 26 that you just read require that an expert witness provide the opposing side with an expert report detailing the basis for the witness's opinions and likely testimony, and they address when the report must be provided. And again, under the Federal Rules, a party need not disclose the existence of an expert witness, and produce a corresponding expert report, until many months (perhaps even years) after the filing of the complaint.

## Background Law (for Questions 2–4)

First, a statement of some tort law concepts that may or may not exist outside of this exam, but that you should assume to be true for the purposes of this exam.

- If the directors and officers of a corporation make an unreasonable decision that subjects a corporation to the potential for substantial legal liability, such as buying worthless bonds or operating a business without a needed license, a shareholder of that corporation can file what is called a "shareholder's derivative" suit. The theory of a shareholder's derivative suit is that an unreasonable decision potentially subjecting the corporation to liability hurts the corporation and thus the value of the shares the shareholders own. Thus, directors and officers should have to pay the corporation (and, indirectly, the shareholders) damages. The result: plaintiff shareholders can sue in a lawsuit against the directors and officers (meaning, for the purposes of this exam, the chief executive officer and the members of the board) for the decreased value of their shares due to the defendants' unreasonable decision. Every state relevant to this exam has this law.

- If a corporation (through any of its employees) causes harm to another person by failing to act with ordinary or due care under the circumstances, it can be sued for a tort called "negligence." To prevail on a negligence claim the plaintiff must show: (1) The Defendant had a duty of care as to the Plaintiff, (2) that duty was breached, (3) that breach caused the plaintiff's injuries; and (4) damage resulted. Every state relevant to this exam has this law. Some states allow for punitive damages in negligence claims. In other states, a plaintiff is entitled to punitive damages only if it shows that the defendant acted recklessly, meaning with unreasonable disregard of a known risk. More on this below.

PDP jurisprudence is about whether to provide greater procedural protections, such as a pre-deprivation live hearing (in Matthews) or notice, an opportunity to present evidence (Restatement 82), the requirement of or opportunity to post a bond (Doehr), or a written decision (Vitek).  The point of all this process is to protect substantive rights.  Certain kinds of additional procedural protection may be impossible to achieve without additional procedural complication.  A hearing (Goldberg) provides an opportunity to present critical evidence, but presenting evidence may mean compliance with well-intentioned but complex rules.  Participating in a proceeding that ends with a written decision  (Vitek, R52) might entail proposing findings of fact and conclusions of law as to what the written decision should say, which are hard to do.  And reading a written decision, with no one there to answer questions and explain, is hard to do.   Even putting aside the money required, posting a bond means understanding what a bond is for and how to access the market for one.  If the complication means that those without lawyers cannot use the process, they cannot protect their substantive rights.  The additional process can be self-defeating.  A key point:  All this is before one takes into account any efficiency or cost concerns.  This discussion need not be about efficiency.

In Turner, the court tells us that when the stakes are high enough, a state must either DECREASE procedural complication (difficult to do if PDP trappings are constitutionally demanded) or provide lawyers. In defense of the proposal, if one is trying to equalize power, one can level up (with civil Gideon, ridiculously too expensive, Barton & Bibas) or level down, which is what this proposal tries to do.  And perhaps leveling down, by forcing the one-percenters in many cases to experience the justice system that the other 99% currently experience, would create a coalition for improvement of the justice system.
